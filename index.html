How to use these notes

These notes capture the teaching from seven days in Israel, September 9–15, 2026: Jeremy (our teaching pastor and scholar), Opher (our Israeli guide), Pastor Benito, Isa (our local guide in Bethlehem) and Yanai (our guide in the Western Wall Tunnels). They come from about 22 hours of audio recorded on the trip, organized into detailed, easy-to-scan notes by day and by stop. Days are numbered by the trip itinerary: Days 1 and 2 were travel and arrival, so the teaching runs from Day 3 (Wed, Sep 9) through Day 9 (Tue, Sep 15).

  • Format: each stop is broken into topics with bulleted notes, with a few memorable lines quoted directly. Side conversations are left out, and place names and scripture references are corrected where the recording garbled them.
  • On the bus: a lot of the teaching happened in transit. Those parts are labeled On the bus and placed between the stops they connected.
  • Gaps: some stops weren't recorded, because the mic came unplugged or the recorder wasn't running, and a few recordings start after a talk had begun or cut off before it ended. Each day lists what's missing under Not recorded.
  • Recorder left running: a few recordings kept going after the teaching ended. Only the teaching is included here.
  • Matching the audio: each stop lists the recording it came from (for example, Day 8-07), so you can go back and listen. Tap a recording label to play it (audio is hosted in a shared Google Drive folder).

Day 3 — Wednesday, Sep 9: Coastal Plain, Caesarea Maritima, Mount Carmel (Muhraqa)

We drove north along the coastal plain to Caesarea Maritima, then climbed Mount Carmel to the Muhraqa overlook above the Jezreel Valley before heading to the Sea of Galilee for the night.

Not recorded: Paul's trial site at Caesarea; most of the Temple of Augustus and Roma talk (only the closing application survives); and the first few minutes of Opher's Mount Carmel overlook talk (the history of armies crossing the valley is almost entirely inaudible). The Acts 12, harbor and Cornelius talks at Caesarea are missing their openings.

On the bus — to Caesarea

The gospel spreads — and then stalls (Acts 1, Acts 13)

  • Jesus said the gospel would start in Jerusalem, then go to Judea, then Samaria, then the ends of the earth — and the book of Acts follows that progression (Acts 1:8).
    • Acts 2: it spreads like wildfire in Jerusalem through the baptism of the Holy Spirit, then out to Judea and Samaria.
  • Then, apart from some scattering during Saul's persecution, it stops — the church waits about 15 years instead of sending the gospel out as commanded.
  • The Holy Spirit moves them to act: "I have set you apart for this" (Acts 13:2).
  • Paul understood he was called not just on the road to Damascus but "every step of the way" for a purpose God destined for his life.
  • Barnabas and Saul leave Antioch not for some distant foreign land — they go "home," because Barnabas is from Cyprus.
    • The gospel returns to a place it should have gone as soon as Barnabas received it.

Why we're here

  • "You did not choose to come here. The Holy Spirit motivated you to come here."
  • Something about this journey will impact not just your life but the kingdom as a whole.
  • The focus: when I get back from this trip, how am I a better disciple — and, as a disciple, how do I make disciples?
  • Listen constantly for the whispers of the Holy Spirit to understand why he called you to this land.
  • God promised this land to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob — but the promise extends to all who are grafted in, as Paul says, who seek to do God's will and build his kingdom.
  • The church rightly focuses on mission, but sometimes God's call isn't to send others out — it's to train you up for mission in your own backyard, where you grew up, where you're comfortable.
  • He closed in prayer for the trip, asking that we go home stronger disciples and a stronger church "whether in Round Rock or to the ends of the earth."

The coastal plain and the Philistines

  • We're driving north on the coastal plain — inland it is lush, fertile and flat.
  • That flatness is what let the Philistines control this area.
  • Israel's history as a nation:
    • The united monarchy: Saul, David, Solomon.
    • The divided monarchy: after Solomon, his son Rehoboam "listens to his young, dumb friends" — red flag number one — and the kingdom splits into north and south (1 Kings 12).
    • We'll deal with the northern kingdom at Mount Carmel today.
  • The Philistines aren't a big issue in the divided monarchy, but they are Israel's number one enemy in the days of the judges, Saul and David.
  • They weren't originally from this land — they were imports from Greece, bringing advanced technology.

Chariots: the ancient tanks

  • Put a few archers on a chariot, add spikes, roll through a battlefield — no one can get near it while arrows rain down.
  • Armies were often measured not by soldiers but by chariots.
    • 100,000 soldiers against 20,000 soldiers with 5,000 chariots: "Guess who's going to win that battle? It's going to be the chariots."
  • So for most of Saul's reign Israel could only hold the hill country — you can't get chariots up there.
  • The Philistines kept scheming to force Israel to fight elsewhere or fight in unconventional ways.

Goliath and Greek-style warfare (1 Samuel 17)

  • The Philistines propose: send out your champion and we'll send out ours.
  • "That's how Greeks do battle" — like the Iliad or the Odyssey, Greek heroes against Trojan heroes, one on one.
  • Ancient audiences of the Bible already knew these things; learning them adds depth to the stories, starting at our first site today.

Caesarea Maritima

Listen

Why James was killed by the sword (Acts 12)

  • Acts 12:2: James, the brother of John, is put to death by the sword — a detail that would have made Theophilus pause, though we miss it.
  • James and John were sons of Zebedee, a well-known family.
    • At Jesus' trial, Peter is outside by the fire; John is the one present, because his family was known to the high priest (John 18:15–16).
  • James is a Jewish man being executed by a Jewish king. How did Judaism execute, all the way back to the Old Testament? Stoning — as with Stephen, stoned just outside the temple.
  • Who executes by the sword? Romans — they beheaded their own.

The Mishnah and "fences around the Torah"

  • The Mishnah tells us much about first-century customs; it was written about a hundred years later.
  • After the Bar Kokhba revolt in the second century AD, the furious Romans expelled the Jews from Jerusalem; Jewish life regrouped up in Tiberias, where we'll be at the end of today.
  • There Judah the Prince wrote down the oral Torah — Judaism's developing understanding of the law.
  • The world changes over a long history: commandments given around 1300–1250 BC weren't all directly relevant by AD 1.
    • "You shall not boil a young goat in its mother's milk" — a Canaanite fertility practice, and the Canaanites were long gone.
  • So how do you keep God's law? Build fences, or hedges, around it so you never drift back into sinful practices.
    • That's how you get from the milk command to: don't mix meat and dairy at all. "No cheeseburgers, let alone bacon cheeseburgers."
  • The Mishnah likewise adds rules on execution: an apostate is executed in the manner of the Gentiles.
  • So James was identified as an apostate and killed the Roman way to show how completely they rejected him.
    • His "apostasy": proclaiming Jesus as Messiah and living it so powerfully that this is how they tried to silence him and denigrate his memory.

Herod Agrippa's death in this theater (Acts 12:19–23)

  • Herod went down from Judea to Caesarea; Tyre and Sidon, dependent on his food supply, sought peace through Blastus, his trusted servant.
  • Today's sound booth was the most important seat in the house — the best view, where a throne would be set.
  • Where do you make a speech in a city? The theater, where the acoustics are perfect — this is the place described.
  • Josephus says outright that it happened here, and adds details:
    • His royal robes were woven with silver.
    • It happened in the morning — "look where the sun is, it has just come up" — so as he stood, the sun hit the silver and he glowed.
  • He is struck by an angel of the Lord and eaten by worms; Josephus says the same thing.
  • Opher added: Herod probably died right here, and James was executed down there. There are many Herods in the Bible — this is not Herod the Great but his descendant Herod Agrippa.

Same event, different sources

  • Biblical, Roman, Greek and Persian sources sometimes tell the same story with different details.
  • Best example — the Assyrian siege: an angel of the Lord strikes down 185,000 Assyrian soldiers (2 Kings 19:35).
    • Josephus: a plague in the Assyrian camp wiped out the same number.
    • Herodotus: rats came among the camp.
    • "Same story with a different understanding."
  • In Scripture we're seeing history from a theological understanding, and we see God's hand on every step.
  • Q: How did Herod get worms?
    • Likely already in his system — trichinosis from tainted pork, a sign he wasn't fully keeping Jewish ways. A very common way to die; Herod the Great died the same way.
    • Some commentaries say the stress and exertion of the moment exacerbated it.

Theater, amphitheater and hippodrome (Opher)

  • A theater is for shows; the Colosseum has no stage but an arena — "arena" literally means sand — for fighting and animals.
    • "Theater for shows and amphitheater for blood."
  • A hippodrome — "hippos" is horse; "the way of the horses" — is for chariot races, the main attraction.
  • The Colosseum held about 70,000; a hippodrome, about 200,000.
  • The Roman strategy: give the masses games, a distraction from daily life.
  • Rome assumed the Jews would eventually accept that the Roman way was right, like everyone else they conquered. They miscalculated: the harder Rome pushed, the more the Jews rebelled — endless rebellions.
  • This is the world Jesus comes into: heavy taxes, foreign occupation, and Jews actively looking for a military leader to get them out.

Herod's harbor and frankincense (Opher)

  • Herod took a new invention and used it in a way no one had thought of — by putting it in the water — and built one of the largest harbors of its day.
  • Ships arriving around October–November were stuck for the whole winter:
    • They paid port taxes, needed their boats repaired, and their crews spent money with local sellers.
  • His main export: frankincense, the hottest commodity of the ancient world.
    • A specific group of Bedouin knew how to move it from the deserts of Yemen to this land, and Herod controlled the whole export industry.
  • This made him "filthy rich" — the source of his money to build and build and build.
  • The builders were not slaves; they were paid — around 10,000 people on a single project — so people could feed their children.
  • An illegitimate king (not a son of David, as far as the Israelites were concerned) stays in power through economic prosperity: "As long as we can feed our children, we know it could be worse."
  • It got bad when his sons took over — "not very smart rulers" — the economy slid, and with an illegitimate king and hated Romans: "Let's rebel, rebel, rebel."

Herod the builder and Greek ideals of beauty (Opher)

  • Herod lived in the Roman period but held Greek ideals of beauty — a gift to archaeologists.
    • In Greek-style building, one piece of a column tells you its full height, its capital, the spacing to the next column, and how many stood in the street.
    • Everything is symmetrical: if one side is built like this, the other side matches.
  • Greek vs. Roman:
    • A Greek statue is perfect front and back; a Roman statue is perfect in front, with a rough block behind — "if you're not going to see it anyway, why waste time?"
    • Greek columns are all one material; Roman columns match in size but might mix marble with a basalt column reused from 2,000 years earlier.
  • Herod was the greatest builder of all time; everywhere he went, even briefly, he built himself at least one palace — Masada has two.
  • His palace here is just below us; the column pieces are reconstructions stamped 1997.
  • Columns aren't single stones but stacked drums, "kinda like Lego pieces" — a hole on top of one, a peg on the bottom of the next — then plastered to look like one stone.
    • Anyone wanting a Samson picture between the columns is welcome — "Samson was a big guy, so he had a good reach."
  • This theater was the first Roman theater in the land of Israel — Roman culture penetrating the land.

Why the governor was in Jerusalem at Passover (Opher)

  • Three times a year, hundreds of thousands of Jews made pilgrimage — not to Caesarea but to Jerusalem, where the temple was.
  • Like the Macy's parade: does the police commander watch from his couch, or is he on the ground making sure it goes smoothly?
  • So whoever ruled — governor or king — lived here most of the year but went up to Jerusalem for the feasts.
  • That's why, at Jesus' trial, the governor was there and not here: it was one of those pilgrimages.

Public latrines (Opher)

  • Running water flowed underneath the seats, with fresh water in front.
  • You used a stick with a cloth on it to clean yourself — something found everywhere in the ancient world. "Day-to-day life. You can't escape it."
  • Q (on the walk to the hippodrome): Were there Jews who embraced the culture?
    • Yes, many — called Hellenized, from the Greek; almost everything in the Roman world came from the Greeks first.

The hippodrome by the sea (Opher)

  • Being so close to the sea, the water kept eating away at the foundations.
  • Herod maintained it anyway because he wanted to show off the beauty of the sea.
    • "This is not like a theater. This is only a hundred percent for fun."
    • He left out the seats on the sea side so dignitaries could watch the ships come and go; normally both sides would have equal seating.
  • Later Roman governors didn't want to maintain it and let it be destroyed, then built another hippodrome inland beyond the sea's reach.
    • Archaeologists know where it is and intend to dig it up once funds are raised — archaeology is not only about what you find; "you need money to start the operation."

Herod's games and the first three medals (Opher)

  • When Herod finished building the city, he wanted to celebrate by reinstating the Olympics.
    • The games had been banned in Rome for about a hundred years because too many people died — sometimes they were to the death.
    • They were held every five years, not every four.
  • The problem: top athletes wouldn't come. This was the border between Rome and its biggest enemy, the Parthians (Persians) — constant war, constant Jewish rebellion — and there was only one prize.
  • Herod wrote back: "I'll make it worth your while" — there would be three winners.
    • The idea of gold, silver and bronze in a single event starts right here, and the prizes had cash value.
    • "We are not the people of sports, but we revolutionized sports history."
  • Q: What time of day were the races?
    • Almost everything was by day — no electricity — from morning to evening.
    • The theater sits at the edge of the city, away from the shopping, so you stay focused on the show; at the hippodrome, "spend as much money as you can, lose as much money as you can."
  • Q: Did the theater and hippodrome run at the same time?
    • Eventually, yes. Early on, when Rome first enters a place, the theater matters most, to teach the stories of the gods and the culture; once people are "already Roman," they can choose either.

Cornelius and the Acts 15 council

  • Cornelius and his whole household are filled with the Holy Spirit (Acts 10); so are Gentiles on Cyprus and in southern Turkey during Paul's travels.
  • The church has to pause: if the gospel and the Spirit are for the Gentiles too, do Gentiles have to become Jews — be circumcised, eat kosher, dress the same way?
  • The Acts 15 council debates it and answers no: their ancestors didn't stand at Sinai with ours and never made that covenant.
  • What they must keep is rooted in the covenant with Noah that binds all mankind — three main things (Acts 15:20, 29):
    • Abstain from sexual immorality.
    • Abstain from idolatry, the worship of false gods.
    • Abstain from strangled animals, or the shedding of innocent blood.

The new self

  • We read those three and think, "That's easy — I don't do any of those."
  • But those three things defined the Gentile world. The call was to forsake your entire old identity.
  • We proclaim this at baptism — I go into the water and come out a new creation — but do we live it?
    • "Sometimes the world doesn't see any difference between pre-Christ us and us today."
  • The identity that made me me "needs to die and needs to be crucified along with Christ."
  • That was not an easy call for the first-century church, and it isn't today.
  • Demas: "Demas has deserted me, because he loved this world" (2 Timothy 4:10).
    • Demas traveled with Paul. In Ephesus, Paul performed "extraordinary miracles" (Acts 19:11) — meaning healing, casting out demons and raising the dead were the usual.
    • Demas witnessed the unusual and still loved the world enough to walk away.
  • That's the danger when we don't get rid of the old self.

Temple of Augustus and Roma

  • Some sources argue the temple here honored not just Caesar but Caesar and Roma — common in the first century, with Caesar as the living-god personification of Rome.
  • Q: Are these ruins the palace?
    • No — the palace is not up here; these are fortifications from the Crusader era.

Application: What's our excuse?

  • The early believers carried the gospel out of that court down there, in the middle of everything this place represented.
  • "If they are willing to carry the gospel in such darkness... then what's our excuse in the world that we live in?"

The view from Muhraqa (Opher)

  • The Mongols swept across Asia from east to west and took even this land, before being beaten by the Mamluks.
  • We are probably at the spot where Elijah's contest happened — whether on this peak or the next one, you have to be able to see both the peak and the Kishon below, which is why the church was built here.
  • The Elijah statue at the entrance shows the false prophets under his feet as he raises his sword.
  • The story is 1 Kings 18 — and Armageddon is Revelation 16:16.
  • Mount Tabor, the round mountaintop: in ancient Israel it marked the border between three tribes; one tradition places the Transfiguration there, when Moses and Elijah appear beside Jesus.
  • The Jezreel Valley holds "endless" biblical stories, especially from the book of Judges.

Passes, highways and Megiddo (Opher)

  • Megiddo sits at the southern edge of this mountain range.
  • The Carmel range is a natural barrier on the number one highway of the ancient world; to cut across, you went through the pass here or the main one next to Megiddo.
  • Whoever controlled these mountains controlled the road — and its taxes.

Yokneam and the Levites (Opher)

  • The tree-covered hill with nothing built on it, between the small houses and the larger buildings, is the biblical Levitical city of Yokneam (Joshua 21:34), no longer being excavated.
  • In ancient terms, a place with a wall around it is a city — even if it's four families.
  • The Levites received no tribal territory, only cities within the other tribes.
  • They were chosen because they alone did not take part in the golden calf (Exodus 32:26).
    • They became God's "ambassadors," the connection between God and the people, serving in the temple.
  • Descendants of Aaron are Kohanim (priests); the rest of the tribe are Levites, the helpers.
    • Even today, the surname Cohen marks a Kohen and Levi a Levite.
    • "It is not something that you can learn and become." Unlike a seminary-ordained priest, no amount of study makes you a Levite — it's bloodline.

Israel's military and safe rooms (Opher)

  • The airbase below is active.
  • Its runways aren't parallel, so an enemy needs two rounds of bombing instead of one; planes land and are "swallowed up" underground.
  • For scale: one US aircraft carrier has more personnel than Israel's entire navy and more firepower than Israel's entire air force.
  • For 20-plus years, every Israeli home has one designated safe room with a blast door, blast window and air filters against chemical attack.
    • His son lives in theirs — it's a regular bedroom in normal life.
    • When the alarm sounds, everyone is in within five seconds; after about eight to ten minutes, a phone app signals the all-clear and life goes back to normal.
    • There are also public shelters, and schools must have them.
  • "It's very strange to see Israelis living through a war" — you go to work, go to school, run to the shelter, then go back to what you were doing.

A land that depends on rain (1 Kings 17)

  • Elijah declares there will be neither dew nor rain except at his word (1 Kings 17:1).
  • Israel knew from the start it was a difficult land — no natural water source for irrigation:
    • The Mediterranean and the Dead Sea are salt water.
    • The Jordan River and the Sea of Galilee are the lowest river and lowest freshwater lake on earth — you'd have to siphon water up, and they lacked the technology.
    • The only source of water for crops was rain (we'll build on this at Dan).
  • The 40 years in the wilderness weren't punishment as we might think — they forced reliance on God, who provided water and manna day by day.
    • So that in this land, when there's no rain, they'd know who to look to and pray to.

Covering all the bases

  • The northern kingdom hadn't abandoned God — they worshiped God and other gods.
    • If the Lord doesn't send rain, maybe Baal the storm god will; if not Baal, maybe Asherah — down the list.
  • Jezebel, a Phoenician princess, pushed the worship of Baal and Asherah while killing the Lord's prophets.
  • Elijah thinks he is the last one; the Lord says no — there are others he has protected (1 Kings 19:10, 18).

"You troubler of Israel" (1 Kings 18:1–18)

  • In the third year, God sends Elijah to Ahab; the famine is severe in Samaria.
  • Obadiah, a devout believer, had hidden 100 prophets in two caves, fifty in each, with food and water.
  • Obadiah is terrified to report Elijah — that's how dangerous loyalty to the Lord was.
  • Ahab greets him: "Is that you, you troubler of Israel?" (1 Kings 18:17) — a strange way to greet a prophet, as if it's all Elijah's fault.

Ahab: the greatest king of the north

  • By earthly standards Ahab was the northern kingdom's greatest king — most powerful, most wealthy, strongest military, most political influence.
  • He was the first king of Israel to master chariots.
    • He had his capital at Samaria and a second capital here in the Jezreel Valley to control the valleys with his chariots.
  • Assyria was rising to the north — "absolutely brutal," picking off small kingdoms one by one.
  • The frightened local kingdoms turned to Egypt, the "big god on the block" since 3200 BC.
    • Egypt wouldn't fight (overextended, vulnerable to others like the Libyans) but offered arms and money — on condition it chose the leader. It chose Ahab.
  • Battle of Qarqar: only the Assyrian account survives, and it claims victory — but everyone claimed victory (the Hittites and Egyptians both built victory monuments after fighting each other).
    • Yet Assyria halted its advance and didn't return for a hundred years, Ahab's reign continued, and the other kingdoms survived. Ahab won and became the hero of the region.
  • Then Elijah shows up saying, "You're doing it all wrong. God's angry with you."

Blessing isn't proof of righteousness

  • "Look at how well God has blessed us" — but blessing doesn't always reflect that we're in God's will.
  • God's grace, mercy and blessing fall on the just and unjust alike (Matthew 5:45); his ways are higher than ours.
  • Most of us know someone who's loaded and a horrible person.
    • Growing up in the New York City area, he knew teenage drug dealers making hundreds of thousands of dollars — more than his parents ever made — by doing horrible things.
  • Elijah was the only one standing up and saying so publicly: "I have not made trouble for Israel, but you and your father's family have" (1 Kings 18:18).

Why Mount Carmel: Baal's home field

  • Elijah summons all Israel, the 450 prophets of Baal and the 400 prophets of Asherah "who eat at Jezebel's table" to Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18:19–20) — somewhere within eyeshot of where we stand.
  • Carmel was the border: Phoenicia crept down the coast, and this mountain was Phoenicia's southern border and Israel's northern one.
  • Archaeology and the text show this place was consumed with Baal worship — his high holy place.
    • In the ancient world you went up to worship, as close to the heavens as possible — the same reason they built the Tower of Babel. This is the highest peak in the region.
  • Elijah is saying: we'll go to Baal's home ground — "We're going to give you home field advantage."

Stacking the deck (1 Kings 18:21–29)

  • "How long will you waver between two opinions? If the Lord is God, follow him; but if Baal is God, follow him." The people say nothing.
  • Elijah lets Baal's prophets choose their bull first — he gets the leftovers.
  • "The God who answers by fire" — fire from the sky is lightning.
    • Baal is the storm god; how would you expect him to answer? With a storm, with lightning.
  • Home ground, the best sacrifice, and the answer expected in Baal's own way — "He's stacking the deck against himself."
  • They call on Baal from morning till noon, dancing around the altar; no answer.
  • Elijah taunts them: shout louder — maybe he's deep in thought, busy, traveling, on vacation, or asleep. "This is where I love Elijah."
  • They slash themselves until the blood flows, prophesying frantically until the evening sacrifice — "no one answered, no one paid attention."

Fire from heaven (1 Kings 18:30–40)

  • Elijah repairs the altar of the Lord that had been torn down — so before Baal was ever an issue, this mountain was a holy place where people worshiped God.
  • Twelve stones, one for each tribe of Jacob; a trench around the altar.
  • Four large jars of water poured on the sacrifice — three times — until the trench is full.
    • Remember what Israel hasn't had for three years: water. "Oh, you're talking craziness, Elijah."
  • His prayer: "Let it be known today that you are God in Israel and that I am your servant... Answer me, Lord."
  • The fire of the Lord falls and consumes the sacrifice, the wood, the stones, the soil and the water. The people fall down: "The Lord, he is God!"
  • The prophets of Baal are seized and killed in the Kishon Valley.

Application: Coming to God without coercion

  • The prophets of Baal spent hours trying to make their god do something.
  • In the Greco-Roman, Egyptian and Canaanite systems, worship meant appeasing the gods to get them to do your will — "Trick them if you have to."
  • We don't try to make God subject to our will; we are subject to his. His will that day was that Israel would return to a right relationship with him.
  • Elijah didn't need anything fancy — "He didn't need smoke machines and light machines," or hours of crying out at the altar — just a prepared heart: "God, show yourself."
  • A pastor once said God is insulted by small prayers; "That's not the reality of what Scripture tells us."
    • We seek God's will above fanciness, riches and the things of this world; in his will, there's no need to coerce or manipulate the moment.
  • "All it takes is, God, I'm your servant. Show yourself here. Answer me today."
  • The answer isn't always what we want, but God promises he's with us to the end of the age (Matthew 28:20) and we won't be left without answers if we are faithful.
  • Elijah knew his God was greater than all of it.
  • The question for us: do we approach the altar knowing our God is greater than every power of this world — knowing the deck is stacked against us and being somewhat joyful about it? "Because when God answers, it shows others exactly who he is through our lives."

Why be baptized again? (Jeremy)

  • Yardenit is one of the traditional sites of Jesus' baptism. He was baptized in the Jordan, but the question is where along the river.
    • Some place it down by the Dead Sea, but "that doesn't make sense when it comes to what we know about the first-century world" (more on that when we drove south).
    • There are two prominent candidate sites, one just north and one just south of the Sea of Galilee, and both are ritually pure by first-century standards. Yardenit, just south of the lake, is the one we went to.
  • In the first century, baptism wasn't "once and done." Immersion happened daily.
    • It began as a proclamation of repentance and became a proclamation of belonging to Jesus.
    • In Ephesus, Paul meets disciples of John, not of Jesus, and has them baptized in Jesus' name. They were already practicing baptism, and John's whole ministry of baptism pointed to Jesus (Acts 19:1–7).
  • In the first century, being baptized could be a death sentence: a public declaration of connection to Jesus at a time when following him was illegal.
    • Today we don't face the same danger, but the emphasis is the same: "a public declaration for all to see who it is that we follow."
  • He closed in prayer for us, both those being baptized and those not. Jesus, who needed no cleansing from sin, humbled himself to be baptized and came up out of the water to show his connection to the Father.

From Jericho to the judges (Opher)

  • Joshua takes over from Moses, and God splits the Jordan for Israel to cross, just as he split the "Reed Sea" (R-E-E-D).
  • Israel enters from the east (modern-day Jordan) after about 400 years in Egypt and 40 years in the desert.
    • The land was never empty. It sat on the ancient world's main highway. He called its peoples "the -ites": Jebusites, Amalekites and so on. The exception to the rule was the Philistines.
  • Jericho is the first city conquered: seven days of circling, seven times on the seventh day, with the Ark of the Covenant in front. Then they shout and blow the horns, and God destroys the city for them.
    • Groups from Nigeria, Taiwan or Hungary all join in "Joshua fit the battle of Jericho": "Somebody brainwashed the world."
    • The message of the Bible at this point is simple: do what God says and good things happen; do what he forbids and bad things happen.
  • Judges tells how 11 tribes settle the land. Levi gets no territory of its own, only cities within the other tribes.
    • Each section follows one tribe finding a leader to rally around and fighting the local peoples. At the end the tribe is settled, "and it was quiet for 40 years."
    • The settlement period covers roughly 250 years (judges like Samson of the tribe of Dan, Deborah and Ehud).
  • A pagan is someone who believes in more than one god. Back then there were two options: Israelite (one God) or pagan (many gods).

Saul, David and the Philistines (Opher)

  • Israel sees that every pagan nation has a king and gets jealous. God isn't thrilled but lets them try, and Saul becomes the first king.
  • The Philistines were Israel's biggest enemy. They held the coastal lowlands and brought chariots and iron.
    • This is the first introduction of iron to the region, while Israel used bronze. An iron sword and shield is a battlefield advantage.
    • It was also an economic advantage: if your iron farm tool broke, you had to go to the Philistines to fix or replace it.
  • Goliath's challenge to single combat was a well-known Greek custom (the Philistines came from the Greek world). The champion was a delegate who represented his god.
  • David, the youngest brother, only came to bring food to his brothers. His sling was the kind you whirl overhead, and the stone was almost baseball-sized: "you're gonna fall down like a bag of chips."
  • After Goliath falls, the Philistine army barely fights. From their perspective, "our God lost this battle." "Gods don't die. So he just lost the battle."
  • David keeps Goliath's iron sword, so he now has a better sword than the king. Saul's jealousy grows as the people's love shifts to David, and Saul chases him (we would see one of David's hiding places near the Dead Sea).
  • Between Saul and David, a surviving son of Saul, Ish-bosheth, briefly crowns himself. The Bible mentions it in two or three verses.
  • David is Israel's greatest conqueror, with influence stretching toward modern Turkey in the north and Yemen in the south.

Jerusalem, Zion and the temple site (Opher)

  • David takes Jerusalem from the Jebusites by a loophole: his men go in through a secret tunnel and open the city gates instead of storming them.
  • David wanted to name the city Zion, the source of "Zionist" and "Zionism," but the name didn't stick.
  • "Jerusalem" is the pagan name: Ur Salima, "city of Shalim." Ur means city (compare Abraham's Ur Kasdim).
    • Shalim was apparently a Canaanite god, "so Jews, Christians and Muslims who only believe in one God still call one of the holiest cities in the world under a pagan god's name."
    • Some commentators connect Shalem to the one God, since Abraham meets Melchizedek, priest of God Most High, in that area (Genesis 14:18).
  • Pagans believed in all gods, the Israelite God included, as one among a thousand. Each god controlled a territory, often marked by a northern altar and a southern altar.
  • David buys the temple site from Araunah the Jebusite rather than conquering it (2 Samuel 24:18–25).
    • What you conquer, someone else can conquer back; what you purchase is yours. The Bible stresses purchases on purpose. Abraham, for example, refused to take his wife's burial place as a gift and paid for it (Genesis 23).
  • David brings up the Ark and builds an altar, but he doesn't build the temple because he is a man of war with too much blood on his hands.
  • Solomon (Shlomo, which contains shalom) builds God's house: "the king of peace builds God's house." Shalom means peace, hello and goodbye.
  • They didn't call it the "First Temple," just "the temple." We call it the first because it was destroyed and another was built.

Why "Sea" of Galilee? (Jeremy)

  • "Lake" fits better. Matthew, Mark and John call it a sea for theological reasons, one of which ties to a verse in Isaiah.

Jesus avoids Tiberias

  • About 85% of Jesus' ministry happens in the Galilee, mostly in the "evangelical triangle," yet the Gospels never mention him entering Tiberias.
  • On his final journey to Jerusalem in Luke, he goes out to the Jezreel Valley and cuts back to the Jordan, adding days just to avoid it.
  • The reason is that Tiberias was an unclean city, built on graves even though it was a Jewish city.
  • Background:
    • Herod the Great's Galilee capital was Sepphoris. Nazareth was effectively its suburb, which fits Joseph and Jesus working as day laborers on Sepphoris' building projects.
    • After Herod dies, Philip builds a capital named for Caesar (Caesarea Philippi), and Antipas has to match him with Tiberias.
    • Antipas ruled the Jews but didn't really know Judaism, so he built the city on tombs. Contact with the dead makes you ritually unclean, so nobody wanted to live there, and he forced people to move there under threat of death.
  • Tiberias became a black eye on the Galilee.

Mount Arbel and the prayer for the Messiah

  • The crag we saw driving in is Mount Arbel, which overlooks the city.
  • Pilgrims walked through the Arbel pass toward Nazareth to avoid Tiberias. As they passed, they developed a prayer asking the Lord to send his anointed one to redeem Israel from the Romans.
  • "A psychological game": you see a massive city on the water that you can't enter, and avoiding it adds two days every time you go to Jerusalem "if you seek to follow the will of the Lord."
  • The Galilee was the most volatile part of a volatile region. Rome's prefects were military commanders enforcing martial law, and revolts broke out even before Jesus because messianic expectation already existed.

Other would-be messiahs

  • In Acts 5, Gamaliel defends Peter and John before the Sanhedrin. He lists earlier messianic hopefuls who were killed and whose followers scattered, and asks whether they might be fighting against God (Acts 5:34–39).
    • Jesus' followers are not dispersing.
  • Paul says he studied under Gamaliel (Acts 22:3). Gamaliel headed the Pharisaic party in his day, and his grandfather Hillel is seen as a founder of one of the main Pharisaic schools (continued at Chorazin).

How the site was found

  • The modern town nearby is Migdal. A Catholic order (he thought the Franciscans) bought the land to build a hotel.
  • In Israel you must run an archaeological dig before you build anything, "because sometimes you find something so important to somebody out there in the world that your entire building plans are going to have to change."
  • The survey turned up ancient Magdala, including one of the earliest synagogues in the world. Today the site is entered through the finished hotel.

The Magdala Stone and the menorah (Jeremy)

  • Everything stopped when they found this ornately carved stone, because its carving connects it to the temple. The key is the menorah.
  • In Old Testament times, the Ark of the Covenant was the centerpiece of the tabernacle and then the temple. What happened to it, nobody knows.
  • By the time the temple was rebuilt, the Holy of Holies was an empty room.
    • Alexander the Great and Pompey the Great forced their way in and were shocked to find nothing. Their own temples held images of deities and piles of gold.
  • So the menorah, the golden lampstand outside the Holy of Holies next to the table of showbread, became the central symbol of Judaism in the New Testament world.
    • In the land, menorahs show up only in places connected with holiness, so finding one here signaled an important stone, and excavation found a first-century synagogue.

A synagogue Jesus almost certainly visited

  • A few first-century synagogues are known, but this is the first from the Galilee of Jesus' ministry.
  • Matthew and Luke say Jesus taught in all the synagogues of the Galilee (Matthew 4:23; Luke 4:15, 44). "I'd say it's a 99.99% chance that he is here."
  • "If it walks like a duck and acts like a duck": he likely taught in these synagogues before his public ministry too. A boy could read and teach once he mastered Hebrew at about 12, which leaves about 18 years before his ministry began at 30.
  • Only one other site in the land carries as much certainty, and we would see it near the end of the trip.

Magdala's wealth: salted fish

  • Magdala was an extremely wealthy city because of the fishing industry.
  • "How many of us grew up hearing that Jesus' disciples as fishermen were kind of this low-class, poor peasant-type people? … They were loaded." James and John were "two of the richest boys in the whole land."
  • The Hebrew name, Migdal, means "tower." The Greek name, Taricheae, means "the place of the salting of fish."
  • Salt was worth more than gold. Phrases like "worth their salt" and the word "salary" (Latin sal, salt) come from Rome paying soldiers and officials in salt.
  • Salted Galilee fish were shipped to Jewish communities across the empire in Spain, Rome and France. Sources describe Galilee fish in Jewish shops in Rome.
    • Why? The Passover lamb can only be sacrificed at the temple. Outside the land, as when Paul keeps the feast in Philippi (Acts 20:6), people ate salted fish from the Sea of Galilee to tie themselves to home and the festival.
  • The fountain in the hotel lobby isn't a modern recreation. It is an ancient fish pool where fishermen dumped their catch.
  • Mary Magdalene was wealthy. She anoints Jesus, and she goes to bury him, which is expensive. Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus, who were also involved in the burial, were likewise wealthy: "The wealth hangs out with the wealth."
  • Magdala rivaled Tiberias in some ways, but it was a clean city, so it makes sense that Jesus would come here.

What happened in a synagogue

  • The front section was the school (beit midrash). The stone with two grooves is a student's desk for resting and turning a scroll. Later synagogues have nothing this fine, and the building also had frescoed walls and mosaic floors.
  • The synagogue was a public gathering house used all week, mainly for reading and teaching.
    • Prayer was focused at the temple ("my Father's house will be called a house of prayer"), and worship happened at the temple. Study was also a form of worship.
  • The synagogue is the basis for the church today. Paul, the "apostle to the Gentiles," always went to the synagogue first in a new city. Only after he was rejected there did he take the gospel to the Gentiles, some of whom (the God-fearers) were already attending.
  • On the Sabbath, the reader read the Torah and the Haftarah, then gave an interpretation.
    • The Torah (Genesis–Deuteronomy) is read on an annual cycle, and the rest of the Old Testament on a three-year cycle. Because of the Jewish calendar, "it's almost as if you're never reading the same thing twice."
    • The two readings are expounded together: "this means this because of that."
  • Hebrew is written without vowels, so the consonants D-D could be "dad," "deed" or "dead." Context decides, and interpretation becomes a conversation.
    • Example: the traditional figure of about 2 million Israelites entering the land can't work. Nine nations larger than Israel already lived there, and the land couldn't sustain a population bigger than today's even with modern technology.
    • The Hebrew word eleph can mean "a thousand" or a much smaller unit, "a handful," or a squadron of fighting men. One reading makes sense and one doesn't.
  • Translators still wrestle with this, which is why there are so many versions: "we don't always agree."

Luke 4: Jesus in the synagogue

  • He teaches in the synagogues of the Galilee and everyone praises him. In Nazareth he goes to the synagogue on the Sabbath "as was his custom," meaning every week (Luke 4:14–16).
  • He stands up to read, so Jesus was literate. "How many of us have heard that Jesus has maybe a third-grade education?"
  • "Carpenter" is a mistranslation. The Greek tekton means builder.
    • The King James translators were English and had never been to this land. English houses were wood, but here everything is built of stone, so a better term is stonemason.
    • The same thing happened with Samson's "foxes," which were actually jackals. Foxes are solitary, so catching that many would be a huge feat, but jackals run in packs. England has no jackals.
    • "We become people of the text and not the tradition."
  • If even stonemasons were literate, the Galilee was a highly educated region, not a backwater.
  • He is handed Isaiah and reads the Haftarah: "The Spirit of the Lord is on me…" Then he sits down and says, "Today this scripture is fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:17–21).
  • That is a messianic claim, yet nobody calls it blasphemy. "All spoke well of him" (Luke 4:22).
    • "Isn't this Joseph's son?" Matthew has "the carpenter's son," and Mark has "the carpenter himself," or the stonemason himself.
  • The shift comes when he points to Elijah sent to the widow of Zarephath in Sidon and Elisha cleansing Naaman the Syrian. Both were Gentiles (Luke 4:23–27).
    • The message is that the Messiah isn't just for you. He is for all mankind, and the synagogue is furious.
  • The context was tension: people hated the Romans and the Gentiles who forced pagan ways on them (the worship of Pan at Caesarea Philippi, and yesterday's hippodrome where people died for sport).
  • Only one disciple really gets it: Judas (not Iscariot), who asks why Jesus shows himself to them and not to the world (John 14:22).
  • They take him to the brow of the hill to throw him off, which was the start of a stoning (Luke 4:28–30).
    • Stoning wasn't a crowd throwing baseball-sized stones at someone against a wall. You pushed the person off a height and rolled large stones down on them. The small stones came last, to finish them off.
    • All this because he suggested God's mercy and grace were available to Gentiles.

  • "If Jesus is willing to say that, then what's our excuse?"

Details on the stone and the seat

  • The oil lamps carved on the stone's side are knife-pared (Herodian) lamps, mass-produced in Jerusalem.
    • Pilgrims brought them home as the idea of carrying the light of the temple, the Shekinah glory, into your house: "bringing the glory of the Lord from the temple to build your house and impact your family."
  • His former professor at Yeshiva University, Steven Fine, suggests the stone is a three-dimensional depiction of the temple, looking in through porticoes toward the table of showbread and its bread.
  • The stone may have been where the Torah was unrolled for reading.
  • In Luke 4, Jesus stands to read and sits to teach. "Sitting is the posture of teaching inside the biblical world." That's why the 12-year-old Jesus is found sitting with the teachers at the temple (Luke 2:46).
  • One white limestone block among the common basalt probably served as the seat of Moses.
    • It was reserved for the elder, and in some synagogues it was where you sat to teach. Because of its central spot, Jesus would have stood to read here and then sat there.
    • At Chorazin we would see an actual throne-like seat of Moses.

Dating the synagogue: the Jewish revolt

  • In AD 67, early in the Jewish revolt against Rome, the synagogue was torn down and its pillars used to build a fortification wall across the street.
  • His first archaeology paper argued that the first-century general who claimed to have fortified this city was lying. The excavation found the wall: "Got proved wrong."
  • The defenders lost on land, took to boats and lost at sea, then retreated overland.

Jesus' wealthy followers and the cost of the call

  • Beyond the Twelve, a group of women traveled with Jesus and the disciples (Luke 8:1–3).
  • At the trial of Jesus, John is let in because his family is known to the high priest (John 18:15–16). Some of the most influential people were following Jesus, so this was no backwater movement.
  • Compare the rich young ruler: told to sell everything and follow, he walks away sad because he has great wealth. James and John did what he wouldn't and never got to enjoy their family's lavishness in their adult lives.
  • The disciples fail again and again.
    • Mark especially highlights their failures. The young man who flees naked from Gethsemane is assumed to be Mark himself, saying "I also failed" (Mark 14:51–52).
    • Peter keeps putting his foot in his mouth.
    • "But at the end of the day … they understood the nature of the assignment and they stuck with him."

The spring-fed mikvaot

  • These ritual immersion pools were very hard to keep full. You couldn't carry jugs of water from the lake, because the water had to be supplied "by the Lord." One source was natural springs.
  • These pools sit on a line of natural springs, and wealthy households paid top dollar to be on it.
  • They were uncovered only around 2019–2020. When the springs were opened, water flooded back in, and it still runs as clear as it did 2,000 years ago.
  • How the ancients drained them is still unknown (today pumps are used).
  • This is more evidence of Magdala's influence, and of why people from its world, like James, John and Mary, had such influential roles in Jesus' ministry.

Studying scripture in its world

  • Q: Where do some of our traditions come from?

    • For example, shepherds in Jesus' day weren't lowly outcasts. They filled a role needed for the temple and held places of honor, and first-century sources are adamant about that.
    • Today we have far more: the Bible in many languages, more people who know Hebrew and Greek, faster sharing online, and more ancient sources that parallel the Bible.
  • "If you're gonna study the Bible, you're not gonna study just the Bible itself."
    • A paper on Abraham Lincoln also has to cover the Civil War, Jefferson Davis and Thaddeus Stevens.
    • Likewise, understanding Joseph requires Egyptian history, Moses requires the geography where he shepherded, and Paul requires Roman history. That is why Paul can quote Greek and Roman authors in Acts 17.

Fish and kosher law (Opher)

  • Fish thrive where salty spring water enters the freshwater lake. Fishermen learned where fish gathered, and villages were later founded at those spots.
  • Kosher law divides animals into three groups (birds of the sky, animals on land, creatures in the sea), and each has a biological test.
    • Fish must have both fins and scales. There's no "50% kosher."
    • Catfish is not kosher. His joke: "We bless it, we circumcise it, and then we eat it." In reality, observant Jews release it.
    • Even with modern genetics, no animal has been found that doesn't fit one category or the other.
  • A "green" way to think about it: in almost every case Jews may eat animals that don't eat what humans eat. "A cow eats grass, I don't eat grass." A pig eats apples like we do. It isn't across the board, but it points to a cycle of life.
  • Separating meat and dairy came later, through tradition.
  • Fishermen caught only kosher fish by throwing the net and pulling it straight up rather than closing it around the fish.
    • Fish whose scales snagged in the mesh were at least half kosher, and then you checked for fins. A catfish can't get caught that way.

Israel's water (Opher)

  • The lake is roughly 150–160 feet deep at its deepest.
  • Near the baptism site is the dam at the south end that controls the flow into the Jordan.
  • Pumping began in the 1960s, and then this lake supplied drinking water to almost every household in Israel.
  • Today almost all drinking water is desalinated or recycled.
    • Tap water is recycled for fish ponds, and fish-pond water is recycled again for agriculture, such as date palms, which need lots of water but not clean water. Jericho is "the city of dates." There is almost no water loss.
  • Twenty-five years ago, everyone would have put water at the top of their list of issues: Palestinians, the ultra-Orthodox, and politicians on the left and the right.
  • In 1948 the whole area had maybe 1.8 million people. Today, counting Palestinians, it has over 12–13 million.
  • "We're making the desert bloom." More fruits and vegetables are grown in Israel's desert than in the rainy north.

John 21 and the 153 fish (Opher)

  • In John 21, Peter jumps out of the boat in a shallow area close to shore: "He's not walking on water, he's walking in the water."
  • The catch was 153 fish (John 21:11). Hebrew is alphanumeric, meaning every letter is also a number.
    • Any specific number other than a round one like 40 signals a deeper layer, so readers try to turn it back into letters and words.
    • One reading of 153, his favorite, is Ani Elohim: "I am God."
    • A Jew of the time who knew gematria would immediately hear someone saying "I am God."

Calming the storm (Jeremy)

  • In Matthew 8:23–27, English translations usually say a "furious storm" or "gale." The Greek word is seismos, the root of "seismic."
  • Matthew uses the same word in chapter 28: at the resurrection there is a great earthquake (Matthew 28:2).
  • Storms really do hit the lake.
    • It is the lowest freshwater lake on earth and is ringed by mountains.
    • In the evenings, cool Mediterranean air and warm Arabian desert air meet right overhead, get trapped in the bowl, and create furious storms.
  • The lake also sits on one of the largest fault lines on the planet, the Syrian-African Rift. One side of the lake is on one plate and the other side is on another.
    • He cited the 2023 earthquake in Turkey and Syria as an example of how deadly quakes on this system can be, even with modern buildings.
  • So Matthew describes waves, the disciples later mention the wind, and the ground itself is shaking. Jesus rebukes the wind and waves and all is calm: "What kind of man is this?"
    • "Not only did the winds and the waves stop, but what was creating them stopped, even the shifting of the tectonic plates."
    • He shows dominance not just over nature "but over all of creation."
  • This comes right after Jesus heals many in Capernaum. It is what shakes the disciples to the core: "it really is God amongst them."

Application: He reveals himself again and again (Pastor Benito)

  • Many iconic Bible stories happened on this lake. In Luke 5, the disciples have just landed their greatest catch ever. They leave a fortune in fish and resources to follow Jesus, who makes them "fishers of men" (Luke 5:1–11).
  • In John 21, Peter is still dwelling on having denied Jesus three times, and Jesus comes to this same sea and reveals himself again: "try the other side."
    • Even after you've fallen, even after all the miracles, "I want you to know, I am your God."
  • God called people in this group here from very different places: out of Hinduism in a village in India, out of a Buddhist family, from Laos, from West Texas, from New Mexico.
  • "He's not just a God that is out there." He is Emmanuel, God with us, and now he lives in us.
  • The group took a minute to thank God for how he first revealed himself to each of them (at a VBS, through a friend, a church service), then prayed and sang "We Exalt Thee."
  • "The mission is always the same. That we follow him so others can know who our king is and we can be fishers of men."

Herod's sons and the Jordan border

The Decapolis

  • The Decapolis was a third area belonging to none of Herod's sons, running from the middle of the lake's eastern side down toward the Dead Sea.
  • Deca means ten (as in decade and decimal) and polis means city: ten self-governing Roman cities, each with its own governor and tax system.
    • Philadelphia was one of them.
    • The region of the Gadarenes, home of the man called Legion, was another. Jesus sends the demons into pigs that rush into the lake (Mark 5:1–20).
  • The largest Decapolis city, Beit She'an (Scythopolis), was the only one west of the Jordan.
  • When the Gospels say Jesus "got into a boat and went to the other side," it means crossing from Jewish territory (Capernaum, under Antipas) into pagan territory, a completely different culture.
    • Roman cities didn't care where they buried the dead. In Jewish areas you couldn't walk over graves, especially a man heading to the temple who needed to be in his purest state.
  • Jews avoided the Decapolis. There were two routes from here to Jerusalem for the three annual pilgrimages:
    • West, through the Arbel pass to Nazareth, over the Carmel range on the ancient number-one highway to Jaffa (then a Jewish city), then east up to Jerusalem.
    • East, through the Jewish villages in Philip's territory and the mountains of Gilead, down to Jericho (a Jewish city), then about a nine-hour walk up to Jerusalem.

Staying ritually pure (Jeremy)

  • Galilean Jews wanted to stay in a constant state of ritual purity so they could drop everything and go when called to worship at the temple, even if their trade (like pressing olive oil) made them impure.
  • In Jerusalem, pilgrims who had purified themselves received a token to present at the temple, inscribed "pure unto God."

The setting for John the Baptist

  • Before Rome, Israel had its own Hasmonean kingdom, the first self-rule since the sons of David. Because of sin and infighting between two brothers fighting over control, Rome "kind of just waltzes in and takes it."
  • Messianic movements rose: "Follow me, I'm going to get rid of Rome."
  • John came saying that's not how it works. Turn the hearts of the fathers and the sons back to the Lord, and he will send the Messiah (Luke 1:17).
  • John was born to Levite parents who were advanced in age (Luke 1). By the time Jesus is 30, John already has a ministry and disciples.
  • More was written about John outside the Bible by first-century authors than about anyone else of the time, including Jesus.
  • He turned ritual immersion on its head: if you're going to immerse daily, make it about purity of heart and repent every time. That drew huge crowds.
  • Jesus' early disciples were John's disciples.

Could John be the Messiah?

  • John says he is not the one and isn't fit to untie the sandals of the one coming (John 1:27).
  • The Messiah must come from David, of the tribe of Judah. John's father was a priest and his mother a daughter of Aaron, both Levites. He couldn't be the Messiah, yet people were so desperate they were willing to look past that.
  • Likewise, the leader of the AD 67–72 revolt, Simon bar Giora, was proclaimed messiah though his name means "son of a proselyte" (a convert to Judaism), a man of no tribe. He was taken to Rome and executed, and his revolt failed.
  • Paul meets disciples of John in Ephesus, about 1,500 miles away, some 25–30 years after John's death (Acts 19:1–7). Yet Chorazin, Capernaum and Bethsaida were John's backyard, and there's no evidence he left this area once his ministry began.
    • That shows the scale of John's impact.
  • This is why God set Jesus' ministry here. John had already prepared the ground with a people who already had prepared hearts, people constantly seeking purity.

John as Elijah, Jesus as Elisha

  • John is likened to Elijah. Before being taken up in a chariot of fire, Elijah asks Elisha what he wants, and Elisha asks for a double portion (2 Kings 2:9).
    • Elisha is so full of the Spirit that even after his death, a body thrown into his tomb comes back to life (2 Kings 13:21).
  • If John is Elijah, Jesus is Elisha.
  • Elisha raised the Shunammite woman's son, the son he had prophesied she would have (2 Kings 4).
    • Shunem is called Nain in the New Testament. There Jesus raises a widow's son from a funeral procession, and people say "a great prophet has appeared among us." They connect him with Elisha (Luke 7:11–17).
    • "Jesus just went in and provided the exact same miracle that Elisha did in the exact same place."
  • John prepared hearts to follow and minds to see the wonders of God, as Israel had seen them 700 years earlier.

Baptism beyond the land

  • When Gentile churches received the gospel, they were immediately baptized in Jesus' name. Yet outside the land there is no ritual immersion, because you are already ritually unclean. Purity is possible only in the land.
  • So when Paul, Barnabas, Silas and John Mark baptized people, it had become something greater: repentance, and remembering that Jesus himself was baptized.
  • If John's baptism was about repentance, why was Jesus baptized? To show his humanity: he lived as a man, without sin, to create a way back to the Father.
  • In the morning's baptisms, we proclaimed what God has done. "You have a path to God already … but the idea of baptism is creating an example so that others follow and find that path."

Was John an Essene?

  • Q: Was John one of the Essenes?
    • He doesn't think so. John had Essene-like tendencies, as Jesus did, but Jesus also deconstructs some Essene theology, and Paul mixes Sadducee and Pharisee thought. Overlap doesn't define someone.
    • The idea comes from the baptism site just north of the Dead Sea ("brown, murky, muddy water"), where Israel crossed on the way to Jericho, so it already had religious significance.
    • Pilgrims until modern times traveled at great expense, mostly on foot, and Jerusalem was the focal point. Crusaders went first to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre to pray. So in the medieval era a site was set up at the closest point on the Jordan to Jerusalem, and John became linked to nearby Qumran, the Essene headquarters.
    • The New Testament places John only in the Galilee. Josephus, the only outside source, says John was executed at Machaerus, but he believes Josephus got it wrong and that, based on the text, it was most likely Tiberias.
    • "Wilderness" (midbar) here means uninhabited pastureland where wild things grow, like the land around Chorazin, not necessarily desert.
    • John's "locusts" weren't insects. The word was slang for the carob plant, with its beans. John lived off the land, which requires somewhere things grow; the desert wouldn't sustain him.
    • There was little Essene influence up here.

Syncretism: the golden calf and Elephantine

  • Aaron presents the golden calf as "your God who brought you out of Egypt." He blends the idea of Yahweh with the Egyptian goddess Hathor, whom they had been surrounded by.
  • When Babylon attacks, Israelites including Jeremiah flee to Egypt. On the island of Elephantine they build a temple to the Lord next to a temple to Khnum, the potter god who makes life out of clay.
    • The Old Testament also says God is the potter and we are the clay. The similarity led to blending, and about 150 years later the priests of Yahweh were also worshiping Khnum.
  • If you don't keep your guard up, that's what happens.

The seat of Moses (Matthew 23)

  • "The teachers of the law and the Pharisees sit in Moses' seat" (Matthew 23:2).
  • The seat here is a replica; the original is in the Israel Museum. The synagogue is 200–300 years later than Magdala's, and by then the seat of Moses had become an actual throne-like seat for the elder, who explained scripture during the service.

The four "philosophies" of first-century Judaism

  • The Zealots, the "fourth philosophy" and the latest, wanted to drive God's enemies out by force and restore his rule.
  • The Essenes are "the Amish of the Jewish world": hyper-strict, living cut off in their own communities, and rejecting Pharisees and Sadducees as not strict enough.
  • The Pharisees and Sadducees, the two main groups in the New Testament, emerged in the first century BC.
    • After Israel threw out its Greek overlords (in place since Alexander the Great), the family that led the revolt was priestly, and half the people wanted to make them kings. You can't be both priest and king. Uzziah tried, and God struck him with leprosy (2 Chronicles 26).
    • The Sadducees were tied to that ruling family and held power in the kingship and the temple.
    • Those who resisted and wanted pure worship in line with God's law in a changing world found their authority in the synagogue. These were the Pharisees, who developed stricter and looser schools.
    • When the Hasmonean kingdom fell, the Sadducees kept power through the temple, "a money-making industry" that they abused. They were disliked by ordinary people and friendly with the Romans.
  • "The Pharisees have nothing to do with the death of Jesus, it is all the Sadducees."
    • The Pharisees opposed capital punishment and especially crucifixion. When the two groups went to war, the Sadducees crucified captured Pharisees as an insult.

Testing Jesus: a culture of conflict

  • Why do we see the Pharisees as Jesus' main opponents? Because they're who he interacts with most.
  • "Judaism is a culture of conflict, but conflict is not bad. Conflict is how we grow."
    • When Pharisees "test" Jesus, it can be a way of asking to learn from him.
  • His first master's degree, in ancient Judaism and the origins of Christianity, was in New York City. Students from Nyack College (Christian) and Yeshiva University (Jewish) took classes together.
    • They drilled each other hard ("tell me why you think this way"), "and afterwards, we went for Chinese food." They wanted to make sure the Christians truly believed and could back up what they proclaimed.
  • The grain fields on the Sabbath: rubbing grain heads counts as destroying, which is work (Mark 2:23–28; Matthew 12:1–8).
    • This was a live debate between two Pharisaic schools. Shammai (strict) said that if your bull falls in a pit on the Sabbath, let it die rather than profane the Sabbath. Hillel (looser) said to get all your sons and neighbors and pull it out, because God cares more about sustaining life than about the Sabbath.
    • Jesus cites David eating the showbread and says the Sabbath was made for man, which puts him with the school of Hillel.
  • Jesus was very close to the Pharisees, and his message resonated with them. Some wanted him dead, but "there's rogues everywhere."
    • Paul was "the most rogue of rogue Pharisees," rejected by other Pharisees.
    • Nicodemus and Joseph of Arimathea, who provided the spices and the tomb, were leading Pharisees.
    • Gamaliel, Hillel's grandson, defended the church (Acts 5).
    • Acts mentions believers who "are" Pharisees, present tense. Paul says "I am a Pharisee." Before the Sanhedrin the Sadducees want to kill him and the Pharisees defend him (Acts 23:6–9).
  • Like a text message from your spouse, we can misread the sentiment of a text. One sad influence on how the Pharisees are read is antisemitism in the church.

Matthew 23: a human problem

  • Jesus affirms the Pharisees' teaching ("do everything they tell you") but warns against their practice: "do not do what they do" (Matthew 23:3).
    • "Do as I say, not as I do." "Is this a Pharisaic problem or is this a human problem?"
    • Pastors today preach great sermons and fall morally. He cited the TV ministries of Jim Bakker and Jimmy Swaggart in the 1980s: many were saved through them, but you wouldn't want to do what those pastors did.
  • "They tie up heavy, cumbersome loads" (Matthew 23:4): how often do we tell a new believer to do something we aren't doing ourselves?
  • Wide phylacteries and long tassels were markers of a devotional life (more on them at the Western Wall). Love of honor, seats and titles follows. "You have one teacher … the greatest among you will be your servant … those who humble themselves will be exalted" (Matthew 23:5–12).

Application

  • The things the Pharisees struggled with, "at one point we were there too."
  • We need the same salvation and grace, working out our faith with fear and trembling (Philippians 2:12), staying humble, and knowing we're not in control but serve a master who is.
  • Our responsibility: "Lord, how do I be more like you each and every day?"

Antisemitism and the church

  • Q: A traveler noted that some early church fathers introduced antisemitism, and that only in the 1960s did the Catholic Church formally say the Jews didn't kill Jesus. How do we unwind that?
    • We're fighting generations of people who claimed to be Christians and set a horrible example. It won't be fixed in our lifetime.
    • The "parting of the ways" came in two steps. In the revolt of AD 67–72, Christians heeded the Olivet Discourse and fled (Matthew 24:15–20), and Josephus notes they were nowhere to be found during the siege. When they returned they were ostracized.
    • Rome then taxed Jews simply for being Jewish (the Fiscus Judaicus). About 50 years later the Bar Kokhba revolt failed, and Rome persecuted Jews across the empire.
    • To survive, Jewish communities often turned on the small, underground churches. He sees this already in Revelation, where the churches of Smyrna and Philadelphia are persecuted by the synagogue (Revelation 2:9; 3:9).
    • When Christianity was legalized in 313, the church turned on the Jews.
      • In Rome, the head of the synagogue was made to kiss a New Testament once a year at the Arch of Titus, which celebrates the destruction of the temple, "something that as believers we should be mourning."
    • He described the Crusades not as Christianity versus Islam but "my branch of Christianity against everybody else": Crusaders fought Muslims and also attacked Jewish villages and Greek Orthodox Christians.
    • His friend Moshe (whom we would meet later) says every Jew you meet isn't 50 or 60 years old, "they're 3,000 years old." They carry the memory.
    • He pointed to Martin Luther's On the Jews and Their Lies and its later use by the Nazis, noting that Lutheranism was Germany's state church in the 1930s–40s.
    • "There's a lot that the church has to atone for."

Why antisemitism, and our role

  • God promised Abraham a people and a land, and descendants as numerous as the stars and the sand. Israel was called out to represent God to the nations.
  • "When God makes a promise, the world and everything in it will go against the promises of God." If his promises could be thwarted, he wouldn't be a promise-keeping God, and therefore not God.
  • As a youth pastor, a Jewish student asked him whether he really had a special relationship with God. The answer was "absolutely," and we are special too, because God called the Jewish people first.
  • We are grafted into the living tree (Romans 11:17–24), like the grafting Opher had pointed out in this region. You can't graft a living branch onto something dead.
    • So, he argued, replacement theology (supersessionism), the idea that the church has replaced Israel, doesn't make sense. God's plans and promises for Israel are alive.
    • "All Israel will be saved" (Romans 11:26). How to understand it is open to interpretation, but we can't go against it.
  • He said the church's responsibility is not just to recognize this but to stand up for it. He attends a conference on antisemitism about every other month.
    • He said propaganda is shifting world opinion against Israel, noting that by October 8, before Israel had even responded, people were already blaming Israel for October 7.
    • About six months later he walked through Kfar Aza and Be'eri and saw "the lack of regard for human dignity" in the devastation.
    • He watched 9/11 from his fifth-grade classroom in New York and grew up with many kids who lost parents. He cited a poll in the Arab world in which most respondents blamed Israel rather than al-Qaeda for 9/11.
  • He quoted the Holocaust-era saying: when they came for others, I didn't speak up, "and then when they came for me, there was no one to speak up for me."
  • "God's plans won't be thwarted," but the blessings God has for us in this life and the next are tied to the stand we make for his promises and his people.

Was Joseph a Pharisee?

    • He leans yes. Matthew calls Joseph a righteous man (Matthew 1:19), probably from the Hebrew tzaddik, a term used for a group within the Pharisaic class known as the Hasidim.

The discovery (museum film)

  • Two local amateur archaeologists had long dreamed that "one day the Sea of Galilee will give us a gift."
  • After a drought year, walking the exposed shore, they found an ancient nail, then another, then wood, and realized it was a boat almost 10 meters long.

The rescue

  • "As soon as we started excavating it, we were in a sense destroying it." The air was the enemy.
    • The waterlogged wood would turn to powder if it dried out, so sprinklers and people sprayed it with water all day, under a shade awning.
    • Tools couldn't be used near the wood. It was so soft and spongy that fingers went right through it, so the digging was done by hand.
  • The Air Force, Navy, port authorities and others were consulted, and the Air Force even offered a helicopter.
  • The solution was to fill and wrap the boat in fast-drying polyurethane foam.
    • To support it from below without it collapsing, they dug person-wide channels under the boat one at a time and filled each with foam.
  • A channel was dug from the pit to the lake, and the boat floated out: "For the first time in 2,000 years, it was sailing on the waters of the Sea of Galilee." People had tears in their eyes.
  • The next day a crane lifted it onto shore. "One wrong move, and it could just disintegrate."

The boat and the Gospels (Opher)

  • Boats like this may have sunk during the naval battle between the Jewish rebels and the Romans in the Great Revolt. Are there more boats out there? "Maybe, we don't know."
  • It was built mostly of oak and a few other woods, and could hold six or seven people.
  • From his angle, the man casting can't see where the fish are, so fishermen relied on someone else calling out that he could see fish surfacing on the other side. That fits Jesus telling the disciples after a night of fishing to "try the other side" (John 21:6). Even so, the catch that followed wasn't normal, even for a fisherman.
  • A mosaic floor found in a synagogue in the area shows a fishing boat almost identical to this one.

Pilgrims spread the news (Opher)

  • Pilgrims brought sacrifices according to their means: someone better off brought something more expensive, and doves were probably the cheapest.
  • Community members who went up to Jerusalem came home and reported what they had seen, and the community trusted them.
  • Jesus was crucified at Passover, when Jews from all over the world were in Jerusalem as potential witnesses.
    • A report of a man resurrected in Jerusalem at Passover is news "I don't care who you are." Pilgrims carried it home, "and just like that, every single person in the Jewish world heard about Jesus. Did they believe it? Doesn't matter. But now you know."
  • Seven weeks later came Pentecost (Greek, 50th day), or Shavuot (Hebrew, "weeks"). The Holy Spirit came and pilgrims from everywhere heard those Galileans in their own languages (Acts 2).
  • That made two incredible miracles in Jerusalem, the holiest place, associated with Jesus and his disciples.
  • Roman rulers had informants in every community to watch for rebellion, and the buzz in the synagogues was about a resurrected man and miracles.
  • Christianity used the Jewish communication network. That's how, 30 years later, Paul could correspond with Jewish communities outside Israel: through the synagogue system and members traveling back and forth.

From sacrifice to prayer

  • In temple days there was a morning and an evening offering on behalf of every Jew who couldn't be at the temple.
  • After the temple was destroyed, the rabbis agreed that prayer is a legitimate temporary replacement for sacrifice, and that you don't need to be in Jerusalem to pray. "That was the miracle": they didn't agree on how to pray, but they agreed on that.
  • Since people already gathered in the synagogue, prayer moved there. Jews pray toward Israel from abroad, toward Jerusalem from within Israel, and toward the temple site from within Jerusalem.
  • In Jewish tradition, the foundation stone was the first thing created on earth. It is where Abraham went to sacrifice his son, what David bought from Araunah, where the temple was built and where the Ark stood.
  • "Today, synagogues are houses of worship and prayer, but that's not how they started."

Reading and arguing the text

  • Jesus stood to read and sat to teach (Luke 4), and then came the discussion: "Do you agree with me? If you don't agree with me … can you back it up with something from another scroll?"
  • Jeremy's Luke 4 reading seemed to stop a few words short compared with our Bibles. That's not a mistake: Jesus knew the text by heart, and where you start and stop is exactly what people argued about.
  • Ancient scrolls (such as the Dead Sea Scrolls) have no chapters or verses, only paragraph breaks, and no punctuation.
    • "Let's eat Grandpa" versus "Let's eat, Grandpa."
    • Rabbis debate where to put the commas. Yeshiva University still studies the unpunctuated text for this reason.
  • A great rabbi is one who can convince other rabbis that his reading is more right.
    • "Jewish rabbis are never wrong. They're just not always right."
  • Practical questions went to the local rabbi rather than the temple. For example, if your ox gores my ox's eye, does "an eye for an eye" mean taking the other ox's eye, or monetary compensation? First agree on where the commas go, then interpret. If you don't like one rabbi's answer, go to another.

How a rabbi chose disciples

  • "Rabbi" means teacher, and to be a teacher you need students.
  • Around age 12–13, rabbis visited communities and synagogues and quizzed the boys. They had to know the text instantly: "In the beginning…" "…God created."
  • Opher played it out with us:
    • "What's two plus two?" Four is correct, but not what he's looking for, so go learn your father's trade.
    • A father had two duties: teach his son the text, and give him a trade. "There is absolutely no shame in going and doing your father's work."
    • The right response is a question that shows you get where he's going. Asked "two times two," you answer "Rabbi, what is the square root of sixteen?"
    • "I don't need someone to think the way I do … I need somebody to challenge me."
  • Then come two words: "Follow me." It was a huge honor. You said goodbye to family and went for a season of learning, maybe seven years.
  • Jesus called people who had already gone into their fathers' trades, which was shocking. They could still have been only 15 or 16, and they dropped their nets and followed.
  • The Torah is read in a year and the rest of scripture on a three-year cycle. Jesus' ministry lasted about three and a half years, enough to pour his whole "yoke," his way of reading scripture, into the disciples.
  • Jesus was considered a great rabbi. After his answers "they had no questions back," and even at 12 the teachers at the temple marveled at him (Luke 2:41–47).

The centurion who built the synagogue (Luke 7:1–10)

  • Jesus enters Capernaum, "exactly where Jesus enters in this passage." The Jewish elders plead for the centurion: "he loves our nation and has built our synagogue."
    • "Not only am I going to show you the 2,000-year-old ruins of the synagogue, I know who built it."
  • A centurion commanded 100 soldiers through sheer fear, and a soldier feared his centurion more than the enemy.
  • Yet this centurion cared enough about a servant to ask a Jewish rabbi for help, and he sent friends to say "I do not deserve to have you come under my roof … just say the word."
  • This is the only time Jesus is "amazed" in a positive sense. Usually he is amazed at unbelief. "I have not found such great faith even in Israel."

Why this was so hard for Jews to accept

  • Rabbis agree on almost nothing, but some things are untouchable, and one is God's authority: God can do anything.
  • The belief was that you must go to him: to the temple in Jerusalem, with the right sacrifice, properly dressed and ritually purified. Then, if God deems you worthy, it happens.
  • Jesus taught that you don't need to come to God. He can snap a finger, whisper a word or send an angel, anywhere, anytime, because God is everywhere.
  • For over 1,000 years every rabbi and forefather had taught that God is in Jerusalem. Then a man from Nazareth ("what good has come from Nazareth?") says to forget that, a 180-degree turn: "Are you insane?"
  • Today every rabbi will say God is everywhere. "Jesus was ahead of his time."
  • A Gentile hadn't been taught otherwise for a thousand years, so it was far easier for non-Jews to accept. Paul kept failing in the synagogues and succeeding with Gentiles.
  • Gentile messengers were spreading Jesus' name even before the Jewish community heard: the healed man from the Decapolis told to proclaim what Jesus had done, the Samaritan woman at the well, and the Ethiopian official, all even before the centurion Cornelius with Peter in Caesarea.

The two synagogues and Peter's house

  • The white limestone synagogue sits on a darker basalt base. The basalt layer is the first-century synagogue, the one the centurion had built.
    • He didn't lay the stones himself: "He tells them to go into the Jewish community and build a synagogue. What do you think they'll do?"
    • It was large, extending further to the left, with stairs to a second floor.
  • The modern church "hovers" above the traditional house of Peter's mother-in-law, where Jesus stayed. He called it the "Star Trek version of a church."
  • In the fourth century, Helena, the emperor's mother, came looking for the holy sites and hired a local guide ("obviously not as handsome or knowledgeable").
    • For 300 years, persecuted Christians had met secretly at night by oil lamp in house churches (domus ecclesiae), and she built churches on those sites.
    • Church was built on church, several levels in all. Under the lowest layer were oil lamps, and beneath them fishing gear, which fits Peter the fisherman.
  • The exact spot could be off by 50 yards, but that's the tradition, and the church's clear floor lets you look down into the house. After the Catholic–Orthodox split, the Orthodox built their own church by the dock, which was also part of Capernaum.

Houses around a courtyard: "many rooms" (Jeremy)

  • Families lived in clusters of houses (insulae) around a central courtyard. A father built his house, and each son added his own as he married, so the complex grew outward.
  • This is how Jesus describes heaven. The King James "many mansions" isn't what the text says. It says "my Father's house has many rooms" (John 14:2): many dwellings in one family compound.
    • The translators didn't know this way of life, because it was all still underground.
    • "The family of God does what in heaven? Lives together, lives in community." That is the desire for the kingdom on earth too, in unity as the body of Christ.

The roof and the manger

  • Stairs led up to thatched roofs. That matches the friends who carry a paralyzed man up and cut through the roof to reach Jesus (Mark 2:1–12).
  • Some skeptical scholars argue the New Testament was a fabricated story written years later, but here the geography and culture match the text exactly.
  • Feeding troughs built into the walls show animals living among the people. That will matter in Bethlehem: Jesus was laid in a manger, a feeding trough, and "no room in the inn" with a barn out back isn't how the text reads.

Reading an excavation

  • Numbers on stones are for reconstruction. Fallen stones are pieced back together ("play Legos"), sometimes chipped so they can be scanned in case a better fit turns up. A restoration line (to be seen at Dan and Masada) marks what is original in place versus rebuilt.
  • The octagonal church over Peter's house is Byzantine, with three phases, built right into earlier houses, and remains of the house are visible beneath it. This is called stratigraphy.
  • "Archaeology is a science of destruction." To reach a lower layer you destroy everything above, so you map it and make the hard call whether to go deeper.
    • At El-Araj, a few miles away and likely ancient Bethsaida, where he has excavated, the team found a Byzantine church with a mosaic saying it was built on the house of Peter and Andrew. The only other reference to it is an early pilgrim. They decided to stop and preserve the church rather than dig for what would likely be just a simple fishing village.

Where is Jesus' synagogue?

  • Q: So is this where Jesus read the scroll?
    • The white synagogue is from the fourth century, the same period as Chorazin's, but far more magnificent.
    • After the Bar Kokhba revolt, the heart of Judaism moved to Tiberias. There's a story that Judah HaNasi and another rabbi prayed for any bones within six feet to rise so they could be removed, and when none rose, declared the city pure: "loopholes, people, loopholes."
    • As Byzantine Christians built grand churches, Jews may have built this synagogue over Jesus' synagogue to assert Jewish presence.
    • The first-century building would be the local basalt underneath. The white limestone was imported.
    • Reaching it would mean destroying a masterpiece, and given how Magdala's synagogue was torn down, little of the earlier one may survive.

Application: "Don't you remember what I did here?" (Jeremy's testimony)

  • This is his favorite site, for personal reasons.
  • He wasn't a great student and was running from the Lord. His dad offered to pay for Bible college, and he got saved there and fell in love with God's Word and its world ("Bible backgrounds").
  • He planned the usual path of youth pastor, senior pastor, "global following," but every interview went nowhere. The one thing he didn't want was to work for his dad.
  • The week of graduation, his father's youth pastor resigned. He took the job for the summer and sensed God saying he had closed those doors: "I have opened doors that no one can shut" (Revelation 3:7). The youth group grew from 5 to about 75 that summer, and he had two years of "absolute bliss" working with his dad.
  • Then one Sunday in January, after they locked up the church, his father was killed in an accident on an icy road driving home. That night a board member asked, "Well, Pastor, what's next? You're in charge."
    • "Sometimes God has a much farther roadmap than we do." His father had pastored the church for 30 years, and the church could transition because someone close to the situation stepped in.

How religions build on one another's calendars

  • The Catholic Church rests its claim on this verse: St. Peter's Basilica is said to stand over Peter's grave, and Peter to be the first pope.

Hades, not hell

  • Most Bibles say "the gates of hell," but it's Hades, and the two aren't the same in Greek thought.
    • The Hebrew for hell is Gehinnom, or Gehenna. In Greek thought Hades is the whole land of the dead: heroes go to Elysium and the wicked to Tartarus. Tartarus is the Greek term closest to hell.
  • The gates of Hades are the entrance to the land of the dead, which is death. Jesus is telling them the cost of this confession: "If you make this claim, your life is forfeit."
  • Tradition says every one of the Twelve except John was martyred (leaving out Judas and counting Matthias).
  • Martyrdom is real today in the underground church in places like China and North Korea.

From the Crusades to the Ottomans

The Ottoman census and the population shift

  • The Turks set taxes by census: count the people in a governor's region and set his quota from that number.
  • Opher said the early 19th-century Ottoman census found about 250,000 people in Palestine: about 100,000 Christians (the largest group), then Jews, with Muslims the smallest.
  • Ottoman subjects could move freely between provinces and paid taxes wherever they lived. Newcomers meant the governor met his quota easily and kept the extra, while losing people made it harder. So governors competed for people.
  • In the 1830s the Ottomans cancelled the law banning the sale of Muslim-owned land to non-Muslims, because the nearly bankrupt empire wanted the sales tax.
    • European powers (Britain, France, Germany, Italy and others) started buying land. Interest in the Holy Land revived, and pilgrims brought business: guides, transport, souvenirs and hotels.
  • Around 1840 there was a messianic buzz in the Jewish world. Many Jews moved here to greet the Messiah, and when he didn't come, some left.
  • The Ottomans built railways, including a line connecting Istanbul with Mecca and Medina, and the first railway in the Middle East, Jerusalem–Jaffa. That drew Muslim workers from around the empire, including from Egypt.

Biblical months and days

  • Opher: the modern Jewish calendar is very different from the biblical one. In the five books of Moses, months have numbers, not names ("the fourteenth day of the seventh month").
  • The first month is in spring, around April: "This month shall be the first of months for you" (Exodus 12:2). On the tenth the lamb is brought to be checked for blemishes before Passover four days later.
  • New Year's doesn't appear in the text at all. The first day of the seventh month (now) is "a day of trumpet blasts" (Leviticus 23:24), and "even the rabbis are still arguing today... what that actually means."
  • Days had no names. Genesis says "day one," not "the first day," because "first" implies a second. The days are one through six, and the seventh is Shabbat, from the word for resting or ceasing.
  • The only month name in the First Temple period is Ziv, the "month of light," the second month (around May), under Solomon.
  • In Babylon the Jews adopted the Babylonian month names (Tishrei, Kislev, Tevet, Nisan and others), some of them names of pagan gods. Tishrei, the current month, is Akkadian for "beginning."

The Essenes' solar calendar

  • Josephus lists four "philosophies": Pharisees, Sadducees, zealots, and a men-only group by the Dead Sea. Since the Dead Sea Scrolls were found in 1947–48, we know more about the Essenes than about the other three, because we have their own writings.
  • The modern Jewish calendar is lunisolar, with 354-day lunar years. The Essenes used a purely solar year of 364 days, which divides exactly into 52 weeks, so each holiday falls on the same weekday every year.
    • To stay close to the true year of about 365¼ days, they added a week every seven years and an extra week every 28 years.
  • Four seasons of three months each. The rabbis made the first day of the seventh month New Year's, because that's when they say Adam was created: "Can't count time if you don't have people to count."
  • The solar year begins on a Wednesday, the fourth day, when the sun and moon were created. The light of day one was heavenly light, not the sun.
  • Put every dated event in the Bible on this calendar and "not a single date that is mentioned in the text falls on a Saturday." Most fall on Monday, Wednesday or Friday.
  • It lost out because the Essenes were seen as a cult. They rejected the temple once the high priest, who in their view bought the office from the Greeks, adopted the Greek lunar calendar and holidays like Yom Kippur were no longer kept on the right day. So they moved to the Dead Sea.

Our calendar and yours

  • The Roman year started in March with ten months, plus an ignored stretch of winter. January and February were added later, so September ("seven"), October ("eight," as in octopus), November and December ("ten") moved two places.
  • Quintilis and Sextilis (months five and six) were renamed for Julius Caesar and Augustus: July and August.
  • As on the bus that morning: pagan lights for the sun's birthday became Hanukkah and then Christmas. "We're always, always connected with our calendars."
  • Passover and Easter should fall together, but the 354-day lunar year runs 11 days short. Judaism fixes this by adding a whole month seven times in every 19-year cycle.
  • Q: Isn't there a 13-day difference between the Julian and Gregorian calendars?
    • Opher hadn't heard of it. Calendars kept being revised in the ancient world, and "Julian" just means Julius Caesar added his name.
    • "I was also told never to say that I'm an expert on anything, because an expert is someone who knows more and more about less and less until he knows everything about nothing. I know a little bit about many things."

Why religious Jews keep Shabbat the way they do

  • Ask a religious Jew "Why can't you drive to the beach on Shabbat?" and "you lost them at 'why can't you.'" From his side, it isn't a restriction.
  • His illustration: one year his wife asked that for her birthday he bring home one rose and say "I love you."
    • He took three days off, bought a bouquet, cleaned the house and cooked dinner: "not only do I love you, but I love you this much."
    • "God is asking you to bring him a rose. That's all." Religious Jews do all the extra to show God how much they love him.
  • God says to cease from creation, so they examine everything from brushing teeth to opening the fridge: does it involve creating?
  • Creating and destroying are treated as the same thing.

What that looks like in practice

  • Opening the fridge turns on a light, and pressing a switch completes an electrical circuit: "You are creating light."
    • No electrical devices. A TV left on before Shabbat stays on until Shabbat ends.
    • Some take the fridge bulb out; newer fridges have a Shabbat button so the light doesn't come on.
  • A new bottle can't be opened, because you tear the cap from its ring. Open it before Shabbat and you can reopen it later.
  • No bicycle: it creates friction and heat and wears away the tires. No driving: ignition makes a spark and completes a circuit.
  • Natural abilities are fine: talking, walking, running, jumping, playing basketball, singing. Playing an instrument is not, because it creates music that wasn't there.
  • Walking has limits. Every city has a Shabbat line, and walking beyond it could look like going to work.
    • A seamstress with a needle in her pocket could be seen as heading to mend clothes.
  • A picnic with pre-made sandwiches is fine, and so are Monopoly and reading. Writing a letter or a child's drawing is not.
  • The beach is fine if you walk there, but no towel. Toweling off pulls out body hair (destruction), and so does brushing long hair. You air-dry instead.
  • Not everyone is equally strict. Some tear toilet paper before Shabbat, and not everyone pre-opens bottles.

Welcoming and ending Shabbat

  • About 40 minutes to an hour before Shabbat, usually the women of the house light at least two candles, plus (by a modern tradition) one for each child.
    • Two candles because the Ten Commandments appear twice: once "remember" the Sabbath, once "keep" it.
  • A family day with no phones, TV or radio. Special songs greet the Shabbat like a queen, and people dress in their best clothes.
  • The closing ceremony, Havdalah ("separation"), uses all the senses: fire for light and heat, incense for smell, and singing.
  • The only day that appears both in the Ten Commandments and in creation itself. It's the holiest day in the Jewish calendar, and Jews get to celebrate it 52 times a year.
  • The roads are almost empty, so it's the one day you can nearly count on no traffic, even between Jerusalem and Tel Aviv. "Shabbat shalom," a Sabbath of peace.

    • That covered today's Iran, Iraq, Egypt, Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, Israel and Saudi Arabia.
    • They ignored who lived where. They cared about getting oil to the Mediterranean and about water sources, and drew lines on a crude map.

Partition and the War of Independence

  • After WWII Britain and France brought their soldiers home and handed the regions to the new United Nations.
  • The UN sent delegations and voted on their recommendations: independence for India, Pakistan, Egypt, Jordan (1946) and Lebanon.
  • The delegation to Palestine reported that Muslims and Jews "don't get along." Its plan:
    • A Jewish homeland where Jews were the majority and a Muslim homeland where Muslims were.
    • Jerusalem to stay international under UN troops, together with Jaffa (the nearest port) and the road between them.
  • The UN voted for partition in November 1947, with 33 countries in favor and about 11 against.
  • The Jews got no Jerusalem and about 48–49% of the land, and they still celebrated in the streets. Ben-Gurion: "A little bit is better than nothing, we accept."
  • The Muslim population rejected it: roughly 1.1 million Muslims against maybe 600,000 Jews, and far more Muslims in the wider region. "We want it all. Let's go to war."
  • The British were still there, so the newly independent Arab states sent weapons but not armies. The first six months were a civil war between local Jews and Muslims.
  • On May 14, 1948, the Jewish leadership met in a small house in Tel Aviv and declared a Jewish state, with its borders to be decided.
    • President Truman had warned against declaring because the Arab states would invade. Ben-Gurion said they'd invade anyway once the British left.
    • Truman was the first to call and congratulate them.
  • On May 15 the British left. Tel Aviv was bombed and eight Arab countries invaded.
  • Ceasefires were signed around July–August 1949. Israel ended up holding more land than the partition plan gave it.
  • About 1% of the Jewish population (6,000 people) was killed. For the US, 1% would be about 3.5 million.
  • The local Muslim population "was offered 50% of the land" and at the end of the war received nothing.

1949–1967: the Gaza Strip and the "West Bank"

  • Egyptian troops in Gaza held a strip north and south of the city, which became the Gaza Strip.
  • Jordan held part of Jerusalem and the hill country up to half the Dead Sea (the dotted line on our maps). It lay on the west bank of the Jordan River, so Jordan named it the "West Bank."
    • It's a political term the Jordanians coined in 1949 for an area they controlled militarily.
  • Neither Egypt nor Jordan gave the local Muslims citizenship.
  • Jews living in those areas were told, "fight us and die, or get out." Not a single Jew remained in either area.

1967 and Israeli left/right politics

  • In Israel, left and right are defined by how you describe 1967, not by abortion or guns. "You could be a communist on the right."
  • The left says Israel conquered the Golan, Sinai, Gaza and the West Bank, and may have to give Gaza and the West Bank back to the Palestinians.
  • The right says Israel liberated Gaza and Judea and Samaria, the 3,000-year-old name for where the tribe of Judah lived. "How can we conquer something that's ours?"
    • "The word 'back' in right-wing politics is taboo."
  • The moderate right: there are about 2.5 million Muslims in Judea and Samaria. Giving them citizenship would end the Jewish majority within 20 years, and a Jewish-majority state is a central purpose of Israel. So they'd negotiate giving some land for a Palestinian state.
  • His neutral version: everyone agrees Israel conquered Sinai and the Golan. The West Bank was taken from Jordanian military control, and Jordan never annexed it.
    • The British took it from the Ottomans and never granted it independence. The last autonomy here was the Jewish Hasmonean kingdom, about 2,100 years ago. Everything since was military rule.
  • Israel never annexed the West Bank either, so its residents didn't get citizenship. Israeli military law applies there, not Israeli civil law.
  • Jews began returning to communities they had lived in 18 years earlier, and new ones were founded. These are Israeli citizens living outside the state's jurisdiction.
  • Muslims inside Israel who stayed put during 1948 and didn't join the fight became citizens. Today there are almost 2 million Muslim citizens who vote and serve everywhere, from bus drivers to Knesset members and Supreme Court justices.
  • Overall about 7 million Jewish citizens, plus 700,000–800,000 Christians, Druze, Baha'i and others.
  • Q: If I'm born in the West Bank today, am I an Israeli citizen?
    • Only if a parent is Israeli. Very few West Bank residents got Jordanian citizenship. There's now a process for a temporary Jordanian passport so they can fly out and return, but it isn't citizenship.
  • After 1967 Jordan and Egypt said, in effect, "good riddance, we don't want it back."

Honor, Islam and the road to peace

  • Western understanding misses "how important power and honor" are here, especially to leaders. "There is nothing more shameful than losing a battle."
  • Losing to a non-Muslim entity in 1949 was especially humiliating.
  • The hierarchy as he described it: Islam on top; Christians and Jews a level below as "people of the book"; everyone else below that.
    • When Islam conquered an area, non-monotheists had to convert or die. Jews and Christians could keep practicing but paid an extra tax (jizya) as dhimmi, second-class citizens.
    • They could hold government posts but never lead the state: "not a glass ceiling — a cement ceiling."
  • After 1,300 years on top, 1948 looked like a Jewish rebellion that succeeded. 1967, lost in six days, was another humiliation.
  • In 1973 Egypt could finally say "you lost" and felt its honor restored. Four years later President Sadat, "not a very religious one," offered to talk peace.
    • Israel feared a Trojan horse. Sadat came and spoke to the Knesset. His message was "you know I'm stronger than you," followed by a list of demands, including returning Sinai.
    • The 1979 peace treaty was a real peace treaty, not a ceasefire, with the powerhouse of the Arab world. It has held through all of Egypt's upheavals, including the Muslim Brotherhood period.
  • Jordan: the Hashemite kings once ruled Mecca and Medina and claim descent from Muhammad. The Saudi family (for whom Saudi Arabia is named) drove them out.
    • The Hashemites had helped the British, who asked the Jews whether they'd mind the east side of the Jordan going to them, even though Britain had promised the Jews all of biblical Israel. The Jews said yes, since almost no Jews lived there.
    • The UN later halved the west side as well, leaving less than a quarter of what was promised, and "we're fine with that."
    • Ever since, Israel has had a relationship with the Hashemites unlike any other Arab leadership. In 1973 the king of Jordan flew in by private helicopter to warn Israel of the war 10 days ahead.
  • Jordan is small, has no oil or natural resources to speak of, and depends on Israel for security and water. It couldn't be first to make peace, but once Egypt had, the way was clear, and a treaty followed in the 1990s.
  • (Here we passed the security checkpoint into the West Bank: "We are now officially out of Israel.")

The PLO and the Intifada

  • The PLO was founded in 1964, before 1967, when Jordan held the West Bank and Egypt held Gaza. So "liberating Palestine" meant all of the land, and that charter never changed.
    • Hamas and the Palestinian Authority today claim Tel Aviv and Tiberias as Palestine: "from the river to the sea." The Jews could "drown, leave, go to Alaska — just not here."
  • The PLO used violence, and after 1967 it attacked Israeli forces too. This culminated in the Intifada in the late 1980s.
    • Intifada is Arabic for a dog shaking itself to get rid of fleas. Civilians picked up stones, sticks, anything.
  • Gazans also threw stones at Egyptian border troops, who were ordered to shoot to kill. Almost 500 were killed in under a week, and no stone was thrown at an Egyptian soldier for the next 35 years.
  • Israel, as a democracy, couldn't order troops to shoot unarmed civilians. The news cameras came, and it took four or five years to work out and legislate a response. There were far more dead on the Palestinian side.

The Oslo Accords

  • After four or five years of violence the Palestinian leadership agreed to talk, which produced the Oslo Accords.
  • "If you read it, we don't agree on anything": not borders, movement from Gaza, water or anything else. The one agreement was to start with test runs.
  • The West Bank was divided into Areas A, B and C (there are others, but these are the main ones).
    • Area A (autonomy): cities that were already about 95% Arab, such as Nablus, Jenin and Ramallah. The new Palestinian Authority (replacing the PLO) handles everything a municipality does, like sewage, roads, taxes, schools and post, and also security, with Israeli-supplied guns and US-trained forces.
    • Area B: the PA handles civil matters and Israel handles security.
    • Area C: the Israeli military handles both. The road we were driving is in Area C. Israel collects the taxes and transfers them to the PA if the PA keeps its side.
  • The plan was for C to become B and B to become A over five years as the PA proved itself, leading to a contiguous Palestinian state, while the rest was negotiated.
  • Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin was assassinated by a far-right extremist who saw Oslo as the beginning of the end of Israel.
    • As opposition leader, Netanyahu had warned, "we're giving them guns," and that in five or ten years they'd be used against Israel.
  • Netanyahu then became PM for the first time. The Knesset had ratified Oslo, so he couldn't cancel it, and he read it cover to cover.
    • Opher called him a "strategic genius" and a very savvy politician. He has met him a few times: "There is an aura in the room."
    • His approach was to follow Oslo to the letter. Israel had done steps 1–10, and it would do step 11 once the PA did its 1–10. "We're still waiting." When the five years ran out, Oslo became void "by doing absolutely nothing."
  • In 2005 Hamas won Gaza in what former President Carter called a democratic election. Hamas doesn't recognize Israel, so for Hamas Oslo never happened. Today Oslo only really applies to the West Bank.
    • Most of Hamas's leadership has been killed by Israel in the last three years, but it still controls Gaza.

The West Bank today

  • A/B/C still largely holds. Bethlehem is Area A, as are Jenin and Ramallah. It isn't concentric circles: "It's like Swiss cheese," and you can go straight from Israel into A.
  • Two leaderships: one doesn't recognize Israel, and the other recognizes it "but while they're doing that, still wanna kill us."
    • "How do you handle somebody who shakes your hand in a negotiating room while his other hand is pressing a button to launch rockets?"
  • Mahmoud Abbas was elected for a five-year term and is now in its 20th year, because he knows he'd lose. Opher's view: Hamas would probably take about 90% in a West Bank election. So even an agreement Abbas signs wouldn't be worth much.
  • Looking out at the open land: "It's not that we don't have enough space to live together... [we] don't want to live together. Or at least that's what our politicians tell us."
  • Most of the people he works with in tourism are Muslims. A driver slept over at his house before an early trip, they came to his son's bar mitzvah, and he goes to their weddings. "The majority of the populations on both sides just want to live in peace."
  • Tomorrow in Bethlehem (Area A), he as an Israeli Jew isn't allowed in, though tourists are. "You... have more rights and privileges of traveling than I do." Marzouk, our driver, is an Israeli citizen but not Jewish, so he can drive us in, and we'll have a local guide there.
  • Q: Why aren't you allowed in?
    • Security in Area A is Palestinian. Israel doesn't want an Israeli Jew there, because if something happened the army would have to go into Area A.
    • Before the October 7 war, Jewish-Israeli guides could enter only Bethlehem and Jericho (the tourist areas). They had to notify the military, bring at least six people and stay in a specific area. He expects it to reopen eventually.
  • Entering Area B with a group requires a bulletproof bus. For example, the Tomb of the Patriarchs in Hebron means switching buses for about one mile.
  • On the left was the Israel–Jordan border fence, with the Jordanian mountains beyond and the Jordan River in a no-man's land between. Photos are fine as long as you don't photograph the crossing itself.

Holy ground

  • Somewhere on the Plains of Jericho, before the conquest, God told Joshua to take off his sandals because the place was holy (Joshua 5:15).
  • God himself called only two places holy: where Moses saw the burning bush in Sinai (Exodus 3:5) and here. "We are literally traveling now over holy ground."
    • "God never said that the Vatican should be holy. God never said that Mecca and Medina should be holy." People made those places holy.
  • The "wonderful loophole in religion": once a place is made holy, you can't un-holy it. When David bought the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite and the ark came there, that place became holy forever.

Holy to Jews, then Christians, then Muslims

  • Anything holy to Jews automatically becomes holy to Christians and then to Muslims, in that order and never the other way around.
    • Jesus was Jewish. Islam traces itself to Abraham through his firstborn, Ishmael, and sees Muhammad as Ishmael's descendant.
    • The number of Muslims today is, from their perspective, God's promise to Abraham kept: descendants like the sand and the stars.
  • Common Muslim names are biblical: Musa (Moses), Harun (Aaron), Yusuf (Joseph) and Isa (Jesus).
  • Islam sees itself as the continuation of Judaism and Christianity. Both texts are holy, but the Quran is "the final update from God," and the earlier texts had names changed.
    • Same story, same event, and "98% of the time" the same location, but a different character. In the Quran, Abraham takes his firstborn, Ishmael, not Isaac.
  • So when David chose Jerusalem for the temple, it became holy to Islam too, even though the word Jerusalem never appears in the Quran.

The Dead Sea

  • It's about 1,500 feet below sea level and the saltiest body of water on earth. It's called "Dead" because no fish live in it.
  • Prophecy: when the Messiah comes, water will flow from the temple, the Mount of Olives will split, and the Salt Sea will turn fresh, with fish and fishermen (Ezekiel 47:8–10; Zechariah 14:4).
    • With every group he checks whether any fishermen are actually catching fish. "It hasn't happened yet. But that would be a clear sign that Messiah is here."
  • Every drop flowing in is fresh water: rain from Jerusalem and Jordan, and the Jordan River from the Sea of Galilee. The saltiness comes from almost a kilometer of pure salt beneath the water.
  • It's rich in sulfur, phosphates and other minerals that are good for the skin, and has been known for healing since biblical times.
    • Doctors in Europe, especially Russia, send patients here. After a few days the conditions at this depth ease arthritic joints.
    • Many companies make skin products from it. The best known, Ahava, is Israeli but was bought by the Chinese. "368" was named for its depth in meters, which is no longer accurate.

Why the Dead Sea is shrinking

  • It sits on the largest fault line in the world, which runs from Turkey to Kenya through the Sea of Galilee, the Jordan and the Dead Sea.
    • Israel is on the African plate and Jordan on the Asian plate, and they're moving apart. Every earthquake makes the basin larger, deeper and wider, so the water level drops.
    • The horizontal lines at the tops of the cliffs mark old water levels. A big gap between lines means a big earthquake.
  • Evaporation is constant, with 102–105°F that day.
  • Since the 1960s, Israel's dam at the south end of the Sea of Galilee has controlled the Jordan's flow and held back water that would naturally reach the Dead Sea.
    • At first the sea dropped about 10 meters (30 feet) a year. It's now about 5–8 feet a year and moving toward a balance.
  • Forty years ago he could park by the road, hop the fence and walk into the water. "Now look how far I have to walk."
  • In 1908 a British member of the Palestine Exploration Fund rowed out from where the camels were and marked water levels on a rock (marked "PEF" with a small green sign).
    • The black line is above the height of the bus, so the road we were on was under water a hundred years ago. "There's nothing like mankind to completely destroy nature."
  • Because the inflow is fresh, there's vegetation and date fields around the sea.
  • The road is Israeli-built, after 1967. From 1948 to 1967 this was Jordanian-held, and it's still the West Bank.

Flash floods

  • The Kidron Valley, one of the valleys around ancient Jerusalem, runs down to the Dead Sea, and we crossed it.
  • Jerusalem's rain arrives here two or three days later as a flash flood: hundreds of thousands of gallons in an hour and a half, with waterfalls suddenly appearing in the desert. The discolored rocks show where it runs.
  • They're extremely dangerous. A few years ago a group of Israeli teenagers hiking on a dry day, two days after rain, was hit, and he believes eight were killed. The road is sometimes closed.

Date palms

  • They grow for about 15 years, then are cut down when they're too tall even for the machines, and a new batch (visible beside them) replaces them.
  • Bedouin use every part: rope from the leaves, bark for heating, and a trunk as the center pole of a traditional tent.
  • The leaf edges carry a mild poison. A sting swells up and hurts for a while.

Where this fits in David's life

  • David's life has segments: shepherd boy, Goliath, fame ("Saul has slain his thousands, and David his tens of thousands"), marriage into Saul's family, flight into the wilderness, and finally the throne and his dynasty.
  • He had already been anointed, yet Saul had sons, including righteous Jonathan, who was in line for the throne and loved both his father and his best friend David.
  • David came to Ein Gedi because it has water but is incredibly hard to reach. He likely brought only a few men, with others hiding at Adullam. He thought, "Saul would be stupid to follow me here."

The setting of 1 Samuel 24

  • Saul took 3,000 young men to hunt for David "near the Crags of the Wild Goats" (1 Samuel 24:2).
    • These are the ibex we just saw. English translators had never seen an ibex, so they wrote "wild goats."
  • The writer knew the land intimately. It wasn't Samuel: 1 and 2 Samuel are one book split in English, and Samuel dies partway through 1 Samuel.
  • Ein Gedi is full of caves. Later today we'd see the caves that preserved the oldest known copy of the Hebrew Bible for 2,000 years.
  • Saul went into the very cave where David and his men were hiding, to relieve himself (1 Samuel 24:3).

The test

  • David's men said, "This is the day the Lord spoke of... I will give your enemy into your hands for you to deal with as you wish" (1 Samuel 24:4).
    • Notice it doesn't say "for you to kill," but "as you wish." God created the opportunity and was testing how David would respond.
  • Killing Saul wouldn't have been sin. Saul was his enemy, the Spirit of the Lord had left him, and he'd be dead in a few chapters.
  • But David understood God's plans are carried out "not by his plan or his might... but by God alone," and his job was to be faithful.
  • He cut off only a corner of Saul's robe as a trophy, and even then "David's conscience was stricken" (1 Samuel 24:5). Saul was still God's appointment, and David wouldn't force God into "plan B."

David had already seen "plan B"

  • Saul was "head and shoulders" taller than everyone, the giant of Israel, and should have fought Goliath himself. Instead he hid in his tent.
  • Modern translations say Goliath was nine feet tall. Jeremy says the Hebrew text says six and a half feet.
    • Skeletons show average height then was about 4'9"–4'11". Today Michael Jordan at 6'6" would be a short player in the league, because people have grown taller over time. "I've got students in college right now that are taller than that."
    • So Saul and Goliath were the same height. Saul failed, David went, and that's when everyone started looking to David.

"The Lord's anointed" and authority

  • "The Lord forbid that I should... lay my hand on him; for he is the anointed of the Lord" (1 Samuel 24:6). David says "anointed" twice, the double emphasis we've seen since day one.
  • Every authority over us (government, work, biblical authority) is ordained by the Lord, "for good or for bad."
    • God raised up Nebuchadnezzar to destroy Jerusalem as discipline in exile. He raised up Cyrus, a pagan king who "does not know me," to restore Israel after 70 years (Isaiah 45).
  • "None of that is a surprise to the Lord." God sees "a much larger roadmap" than our selfish or temporal view.

David's words and Saul's response

  • David rebuked his men, let Saul leave, then called out "my lord the king" and bowed face to the ground (1 Samuel 24:7–8).
  • He showed Saul the corner of his robe as proof he meant no harm: "May the Lord judge between you and me... but my hand will not touch you" (1 Samuel 24:11–12).
  • David put his fate in God's hands. Saul had dragged 3,000 men into a desert where you can't drink from the Dead Sea, and David had every right. "I want the Lord to be able to work in this situation."
  • Saul wept aloud: "You are more righteous than I... I know that you will surely be king" (1 Samuel 24:16–20).
    • It's the first time Saul publicly acknowledged God's plan, and he did it in front of all 3,000 soldiers. "Do you think that soldier is going to remember that moment? Of course."
  • Saul asked David to swear not to wipe out his descendants (1 Samuel 24:21).
    • New kings normally wiped out the former ruling family. When Solomon took the throne he killed rivals among David's sons and David's general, Joab.
    • David kept his oath. The only sons of Saul put to death were because Saul broke Joshua's covenant with the Gibeonites. Jonathan's son Mephibosheth ate at David's table, and Saul's family became like family to him, even after betrayal.
  • David's character shifts later as king, but here "his heart is prepared to be the leader that God needed that Saul wasn't."

Application

  • What matters more: getting our revenge and vindication, or someone having their eyes opened to God's plan? "Let God be our vindicator."
  • What God requires isn't always earth-shaking action: "sometimes it's simple obedience to let him do what he needs to do."
  • If the government, the president or a senator wrongs you ("added these taxes that I don't deserve"), let the Lord be the judge and avenger.
  • Maybe Saul hunting David was really God's tool to train him in the wilderness for what was ahead.
    • When we feel like we're "in the desert of Ein Gedi," struggling to survive under authority we don't like, "maybe God's using that moment to make us into somebody that's greater than who we are."

    • Yes. Beside this stream of living water, it's assumed David wrote Psalm 23 and many psalms, especially the imprecatory ones, while biding his time here. He longs for "green pastures" because there are none nearby. Sit by the stream and put yourself in his mindset.

Herod's desert fortresses

  • To protect himself, Herod built seven desert fortresses. Masada is the southernmost.
  • Metzada is Hebrew for fortress. The mountaintop is a natural fortress, cut off by valleys on all four sides, and Herod built walls and storerooms on top.
  • The storerooms held enough for five to seven years: grain, dates, pomegranates, carobs, raisins, honey, olive oil and wine.
    • Liquids had a double floor system. If a jar broke, the contents drained into a second container below.
  • A Roman garrison was stationed in each fortress just to keep it ready in case the king arrived. Otherwise they relaxed at the bathhouse. It was an easy command with nobody above you.
  • There's no record Herod was ever at Masada, yet he built two palaces: a private one (by his invitation only) and a public one for judging and receiving visitors.
  • His sons and grandsons didn't maintain the garrisons, and Masada was nearly abandoned.

The Great Revolt and the refugees

  • The Jewish revolt against Rome began in AD 66–67. Some families fled the fighting and took over abandoned desert fortresses. At Masada they may not have had to fight to get in.
  • Titus moved slowly and deliberately from north to south, so survivors kept fleeing south. After Jerusalem fell, everyone still able to move made for Masada.
    • Most of the people there were families who didn't want to fight, along with rebels.
  • To the pagan Roman mind, destroying God's temple should have ended the war. Hunting down every last pocket of resistance shows how angry Rome was.

Josephus as a source

  • Josephus describes the battles and the geography, and his description of Masada is "immaculate." Archaeologists find exactly what he describes.
  • His numbers are the problem. Other historians were writing about the revolt too, and he had competition for readers.
  • He was born Yosef ben Matityahu, a priest (cohen) in the temple. Some of the best descriptions of Herod's temple come from him.
  • As general of the Jewish northern army, after losing all his soldiers he went over to Titus as his historian, taking the family name Flavius in Titus's honor.
  • "Where's the plot line? Where's the action? Where's the suspense?" He knew the ending but embellished: winds changing by divine intervention, round numbers like 4,000 killed here and 2,000 there. Add them up and you get almost a million dead, more than the land could have held.
  • At Masada he's different. The description is vivid, "almost as if he was actually here," and gives an exact number rather than a round one.

The Roman siege logistics

  • At least 10,000 Romans including slaves, plus horses and camels, for a campaign of about seven months.
  • The nearest fresh water was Ein Gedi, where we had just been. Josephus describes caravans of Romans and slaves hauling water back and forth. "The Roman military is one of the most logistical militaries to walk the face of the earth."
  • Masada was the last fortress, and the same methods were used on the others.
  • First they built camps and a knee-high wall with patrols between the camps, to make sure nobody left.
    • They didn't expect gold up there. The "prize" was the people, as slaves.
  • Then they found the weak spot and built catapults, a siege tower and a battering ram on site.

Arriving at the top

  • The eastern "snake path" winds up the mountain like a snake, as Josephus himself describes. But the main assault came from the easier opposite side.
  • We entered through the 2,000-year-old snake path gate.
  • The black line: everything below it is as found, and everything above is reconstruction. Masada was the first place in the world archaeologists used this. With no line, what you see is original, and whole rooms were found still standing because almost nobody lived here after the Romans.
  • Below, next to the reconstructed Roman camp by the cable car station, a much larger unreconstructed square of stones is a Roman logistics camp.

The commander's villa and the storerooms

  • The model shows the northern part, which we walked through. We started in part of the villa of the garrison commander, with rooms around open-air courtyards.
  • More than 20 storerooms have been found. When the Romans eventually took Masada, the Jews had not burned them.
  • The storerooms held not just food but weapons and armor: shields, swords, spears and arrows. Liquids again had double floors, and there's a replica jar for honey, olive oil or wine.
  • The black line runs on one wall but not the other, which shows roof height. No cement was found, so the roof was probably wood covered with mud and re-mudded now and then. The walls were plastered to keep water out.
  • Wildlife: the golden spiny mouse lives here.

Bathhouses, walls, quarries and a pool

  • There were at least three bathhouses in the middle of the desert. This was a Roman bath, not a ritual purification bath. It was small and served maybe 30–40 men of the garrison, where a city bath could hold hundreds.
  • A big plastered wall was "more of a psychological wall" than anything. Anyone could use everything on this side, including the public palace (the building with the wooden-looking roof), but no one went past without Herod's invitation.
  • Quarries on top, north and south, meant no stone had to be hauled up.
  • There was also a pool on the southern side, just for recreation.
  • Less than 40 mm of rain falls a year, and it evaporates within hours. Yet there was water for a pool, three bathhouses and a thousand people for five years.

Building for the desert

  • The entrance faces east, so for most of the day the sun is overhead and behind, which means more shade.
  • Very thick walls keep out the heat, and a high window circulates air. It feels noticeably cooler than the 102°F outside.
  • The walls were plastered and painted, and redone when fashions changed.
  • Why the paint survived 2,000 years: the very dry climate, the fallen stones that covered it (most walls here were rebuilt from the stones lying next to them), and paint applied to still-wet plaster so it soaked in.

Herod's Northern Palace

  • Herod's private palace hangs on the northern cliff, built at great effort and "maybe quite a few people" dying.
  • The reason is a natural wind tunnel on the north side. There's a breeze even in the heat of the day: "He's got the shade... a wine cup right next to him and somebody's feeding him some grapes."
  • It has three levels: a semicircle at the top, a circle below and a square at the bottom. Columns stand about 30 feet high, everything is open to the wind, and there are mosaic floors. "This guy knew how to live." Again, we don't know he ever used it.

The Roman camps and the weak side

  • A square of stones far below is another Roman camp, with the fence running off left and right to the other camps for the patrols.
  • The elevation difference shows it's much easier to climb from the western side, which was Masada's weak spot.
  • The large logistics camp has a square within a square: the commander's headquarters, where he oversaw building the ramp to bring the siege machines up to wall level.

The Dead Sea then and now

  • 2,000 years ago the sea was much wider, the road we came on was under water, and there was no gap. It ran another 30 miles south.
  • A hundred years ago it was about 100 km long, and today it's about 65 km. The loss is mostly from pumping water out of the Sea of Galilee and holding back the Jordan.
  • The Dead Sea won't die out. A 200-km channel is planned to bring Red Sea water, all of it desalinated so the Red Sea's composition doesn't crystallize the Dead Sea.
    • It's a joint Israeli, Palestinian Authority and Jordanian project. Jordan passed it about eight years ago ("the king said we're doing it"), and Israel, as a democracy, takes longer. The World Bank is about to fund it, and he hopes water returns within about 10 years.

Romans, the bathhouse and circumcision

  • Opher: a Roman could accept much of Judaism, like not working on Shabbat, but circumcision was where they drew the line.
    • Romans were taught that the human body is perfect as the gods created it, so to them circumcision was "butchery." It was a physical, not just psychological, barrier to becoming Jewish.
    • Every Roman city and even small town had at least a small bathhouse. They were central to social life.
  • Jeremy: Romans weren't just unwilling, they were insulted by it. It became an identifying mark of a Jew.
    • The Roman writer Suetonius describes Jews in court being publicly exposed as Jewish before the jury. "Do you think you got a fair trial from that point on? Absolutely not."

    • Bethlehem normally had about 1,000 people; with the census he believes it swelled to 5,000–6,000, and every guest room was full of relatives.
    • With no privacy upstairs, Mary went down to the cave to give birth.

Swaddling cloth and manger (Isa)

  • Travelers wrapped a cloth around the belly to keep warm and protect the stomach, usable as a bandage or even a burial shroud if someone died on the road.
    • Isa believes Mary used that cloth to swaddle Jesus.
  • The manger was normally a wooden feeding box filled with hay at the far end of the cave, and Mary used it as a crib.

How the site was preserved (Isa)

  • The Bible never names the exact spot; they follow oral tradition from early Christians who worshiped here, at Jesus' tomb and at Mary's house in Nazareth.
  • About AD 130–135 Emperor Hadrian built pagan temples over those sites — and without realizing it marked them for the woman who came searching: Queen Helena.
  • Helena, mother of Emperor Constantine, came about AD 330, demolished the pagan temple and built the first church in AD 333 — an octagonal church over the cave.
    • It was built by local laborers: "dealing with stone, that's our profession... from thousands of years."
    • The region was Byzantine then: "Greece, Turkey, the Middle East, we were all the same people," with the same traditions to this day.
  • The Samaritans rose up against the Byzantines and burned the churches Helena had built, including this one.
    • Samaritans still exist — about 600, living in Shechem, with their holy mountain, Mount Gerizim.
  • About AD 540 Emperor Justinian rebuilt it (the "Justinian church"); he also built the Hagia Sophia in Istanbul.
    • Hagia Sophia became a mosque, then a museum, then a mosque again; this basilica has stayed a church, which he said makes it the oldest church on earth still in use.

The grotto shaped like the cross (Isa)

  • The church is cross-shaped, entered from the west; the cave lies beneath the east end. Its present shape (much altered over time) is read as Jesus on the cross.
    • The entrance and exit each have 14 steps: "you enter the cave through Jesus' left arm and you exit through his right arm."
    • The silver star marks the birthplace and symbolizes his head; the manger area is the heart, the rest the body.
  • The star:
    • Given by the French; dated 1717.
    • 14 points for the 14 generations from Abraham to David, David to the exile, and the exile to Jesus — 42 in all (Matthew 1:17).
    • A Latin inscription says, in effect, "Jesus Christ was born here."
  • In the middle you can touch the cave's bedrock. The marble is protection, because pilgrims used to break off pieces as souvenirs and blessings.
  • We see only about 10% of the original cave, which was much bigger; it's shared by the Orthodox, Catholics and Armenians.
    • The original entrance was about 20 meters away. Mary would have looked for privacy and the warmest spot — the back of the cave, where the star is.
  • A door at the far end leads to the Catholic part of the cave.
  • A 12th-century mosaic is being restored by an Italian firm, Piacenti, through December; before the renovation the grotto was full of chandeliers, olive oil lamps and ornate icons.
  • Some people leave walking backwards "so they don't give their back to the Holy of the Holies."

Mary in the Orthodox tradition (Isa)

  • "For us Mary is a big deal": she is the Mother of God (Theotokos).
    • In their tradition she had no other children; she is "a virgin before, during and after," and the one called Jesus' brother is his cousin, not a blood brother.
    • Icons show three flowers — on her forehead and each shoulder — for that threefold virginity.
    • The Bethlehem Mary icon smiles, "like the Mona Lisa smile," because she has just given birth; the Jerusalem Mary icons are more serious.
    • She always wears red and blue: human, and bearing the divine nature.
  • They believe she was taken up body and soul, celebrated recently at her church in Jerusalem.
  • Mary knew Jesus' destiny and kept it in her heart. At Cana she effectively told him to "do your thing"; he said it wasn't his time, but "he listened to his mother" (John 2:3–5).
  • There's a month of fasting for Mary in August.

Fasting and church customs (Isa)

  • Lent is 40 days before Easter and another fast comes before Christmas. It's fully vegan: no meat, dairy or fish (fish is allowed on only one day) and no seafood.
    • Isa keeps just the first and last weeks. Many families never eat meat on Fridays, the day of the crucifixion — falafel, hummus, lentil soup.
  • After giving birth, a mother doesn't enter the church for 40 days until she is clean; she waits in the space between the small door and the inner wooden door.
    • After 40 days the baby is presented: the priest takes a boy behind the altar, a girl only in front of it.
  • The Orthodox sign of the cross:
    • Three fingers together for the Trinity; two folded to the palm for Jesus' two natures, full man and full God, "united, not mixed," united in Mary's womb.
    • Up, down, right, left — Jesus sits at the Father's right hand. Done three times, and babies are immersed three times at baptism.

The iconostasis (Isa)

  • The wall of icons (iconostasis) hides the altar — a tradition kept from the Temple.
  • Three doors for the Trinity, with two icons on each side of the central door, always in the same order:
    • First on the right: Jesus as King. Second on the right: John the Baptist, shown with angel's wings.
    • First on the left: Mary as Queen.
    • Second on the left changes to show who or what the church is dedicated to: here the Nativity (Luke 2); at the Holy Sepulchre, the Resurrection.
  • "The icons for us, it's an open colored Bible. We never worship the icon, but we honour it because it tells the biblical event."

Red ornaments, eggs and the skull of Adam (Isa)

  • Olive oil lamps once lit the church; now it uses electricity.
  • The red balls hang all year and aren't Christmas decorations. They symbolize the tomb of Jesus.
  • Easter eggs are traditionally dyed only red (as in Russia, Ukraine, Romania, Bulgaria and Greece): red is the blood and the egg is the tomb.
  • The egg also stands for the skull of Adam. In Orthodox, Catholic and Armenian tradition, Golgotha is where Adam was buried, not the skull-shaped hill at the Garden Tomb.
    • When the Roman spear pierced Jesus, his blood ran down through the earth to Adam's skull, "washing the sins of humanity."
    • Every crucifix must have Adam's skull beneath Jesus' feet, as on the cross above the iconostasis.

The Ottoman era and Islam (Isa)

  • During the Ottoman era much was taken: the white marble that once covered the walls and columns was carried off to Jerusalem or elsewhere.
  • The ascension mosaic: everyone — Mary, angels, disciples — is looking up, but the half showing Jesus ascending was chiseled away by the Ottomans.
    • In Islam Jesus is a prophet; they accept his birth but not his crucifixion or ascension, holding that God blinded the Romans and they crucified someone who looked like him — Judas.
    • Muslims may pray in this church but never in the Holy Sepulchre, which is "totally against their faith."
    • Next to it, doubting Thomas reaching for Jesus' wounds survives.
  • Faces were removed from the 12th-century icons on the columns because images aren't allowed in Islam.
  • Muslims honor Jesus as a good prophet whose message pointed to Muhammad, and say Christians removed the Bible passages that predicted him.
  • The Door of Humility: the entrance was reduced to about five feet so the Ottomans couldn't ride in.
    • Manger Square was a market, and animals (horses, camels, donkeys) were brought into the basilica, damaging it; at the Ottoman peak (15th–16th centuries) the monks, a minority, could only shrink the door.
    • Christians were allowed to worship only in the upper area, with a wall separating it from the rest of the basilica.

Bethlehem's Christians through history (Isa)

  • Their language had been Aramaic and Greek. When Islam came in the 7th century they were forced to become Arab, change their language and, for many, their religion.
  • Those who stayed Christian, like Isa's ancestors, paid the jizya: "You pay me money so I don't kill you," handed over on your knees. "How humiliating." This lasted from the early Islamic era until the Ottomans.
  • The British freed them from the Ottomans, and the most education came in the Mandate years (1920s–40s). But the troubles of 1948 followed: "the British took us out of the bottom of the well and then they cut the rope."
  • Emigration: great-grandparents fled around 1914 to avoid Ottoman military conscription.
    • Chile has more than 500,000 Christian Palestinians from the Bethlehem district; immigrants founded the Club Deportivo Palestino (he said 1927).
    • Nayib Bukele, president of El Salvador, is of Bethlehemite descent. There are thousands in Honduras, tens of thousands in the U.S., and more in Australia, Norway and Denmark. He doesn't think they'll come back.
  • "My Big Fat Greek Wedding" — "same traditions, same food, same drinks, we are the same people."

St. Catherine's and St. Jerome (Isa)

  • The Armenians have the smallest part of the basilica; through their door is St. Catherine's, the Franciscan church.
    • The Christmas Eve Midnight Mass broadcast from Bethlehem every December 24 is held there. The Orthodox midnight service is January 6 and the Armenian January 17.
  • Franciscan churches close from 12 to 2, so we only looked in; stairs lead down to the Cave of St. Jerome.
  • St. Jerome (Hieronymus), a Croatian saint, came about AD 380, lived and worked in the cave next to the birthplace, and died and was buried there.
    • In the 12th century the Crusaders took his relics to Rome's Santa Maria Maggiore.
    • He translated the Bible into Latin — the Vulgate, the "common language" — which spread the Bible much faster in Europe.
    • In the 15th–16th centuries Martin Luther translated the Vulgate into German: "Martin Luther translated what was already translated."
  • Symbols to spot:
    • Crossed arms = Franciscan.
    • The Greek Orthodox Tau-Omicron-like logo stands for Taphos, "tomb."
    • The double-headed eagle stands for God's nature and man's nature; countries like Georgia and Russia use it politically.

A living museum — Crusader art and graffiti (Isa)

  • The Crusaders ruled for about 200 years and built a fortress beside the church.
  • Knights carved graffiti on the columns, uncovered only a few years ago when the Italian restorers cleaned off centuries of incense soot: helmets, a wolf biting a helmet, and more on every column.
  • Baldwin, a Crusader king, was crowned here in the 12th century (Isa said Baldwin II).
  • Emblems painted on columns: a rooster, like France's team crest; armor with three dragons or lions, like England's.
  • Icons of two Scandinavian kings, Canute and Olaf, who brought Christianity to northern Europe and were major donors to the Crusades (featured in the show "Vikings: Valhalla").
  • The oldest part: the original 4th-century mosaic floor from Helena's church, about a meter below today's floor.
    • Justinian covered it and raised the floor; it was found only after an earthquake (Isa said 1927, the last one here) led to floor repairs.
  • "It's not only a church, it's a living museum."

Leaving Manger Square (Isa)

Jerusalem stone

  • By law, every house and apartment building in Jerusalem must be faced with one specific limestone. Israel has four kinds of limestone, and this one is considered the best, "the queen of limestones."
    • You can build out of anything you like, but the façade has to be this stone.
    • The British made it a municipal law in the 1930s, and the Jerusalem municipality kept it after statehood. It gives the city its unique look and atmosphere.

3,000 years of Jewish presence

  • There has been a Jewish presence in Jerusalem for over 3,000 years, since David conquered it.
    • Even under foreign rulers who banned Jews from the city, we know of at least one or two Jews living there. "There has been a continuous presence of the Jewish people in this city for over three thousand years."
  • Old versus ancient: "old" means about 1,000 years old, and "ancient" means older than that.
    • The City of David is more than 3,000 years old. Some places we would see that day are about 3,800 years old or older, because something stood there before David conquered it.
  • Jerusalem is "the most conquered territory on the face of the earth." Opher said he could list at least 40 different powers that conquered it, destroyed it and rebuilt on the same spot.
    • In parts of today's Jewish Quarter you're walking 40–50 feet or more above the original ground level.

The Old City walls and quarters

  • Today's wall is only about 450–460 years old. The Ottoman Turks built it, and it's considered the only complete Ottoman city wall still standing anywhere in the world.
    • It doesn't follow the ancient walls. The ancient city covered much more ground than the Old City's roughly one square kilometer.
  • The four "quarters" aren't equal quarters.
    • The Muslim Quarter is the largest, about two-thirds of the Old City, and most Old City residents are Muslim.
    • Arabic is still the most common language. Since the 7th-century Islamic conquest, Christians and Jews here also spoke Arabic, and some Old City churches still hold their liturgy in Arabic, the way a church in Poland worships in Polish.
    • The Jewish Quarter has about 15,000 people.
  • The Armenian Quarter
    • Armenians are proud of being the first nation to adopt Christianity, before Rome made it the official religion. The first Christian pilgrims were Armenian, and they bought land in Jerusalem and elsewhere in the land.
    • In the early 20th century the Turks were massacring Armenians (the Armenian Genocide, which Armenians compare to the Holocaust). The land here was under Turkish rule, so Armenians here, fearing for their lives, built their own wall around their quarter. Once you're inside the Old City walls, you meet a second wall.
    • "There are lots of walls in Jerusalem, unfortunately."

Gates

  • Gates are named for where the road leads.
    • The Damascus Gate was the most important gate for a long time. It's also called the Nablus Gate, because the road leads there too.
    • The Jaffa Gate leads toward Jaffa, and the Zion Gate opens onto Mount Zion.
  • Near the Damascus Gate you step down to enter, because that was ground level when the Ottomans built the wall. Four hundred more years of destruction and rebuilding have raised the ground since then.
  • A cleaned stretch of the wall looks lighter because it was sandblasted to remove "the gunk of the last four hundred years."

Israel's school systems

  • Israel keeps separate Jewish, Christian and Muslim school systems. Israel inherited the arrangement from the British, who took it over from the Turks.
    • The Turks only cared about taxes: "Just give us our taxes and build a school in your neighborhood."
  • The Jewish system is itself split three ways: secular, religious and ultra-religious. You attend the school in your district.
  • People cluster by community, like Chinatown or Little Italy in New York. There are Russian neighborhoods in Israel, and religious families prefer religious neighborhoods (no driving on Shabbat outside the house, no non-kosher shops). So very few Muslims attend Jewish schools and very few Jews attend Muslim schools. It's a matter of where people live, not law.
  • Integrated schools (from about the last 15 years)
    • Jewish, Christian, Muslim, Druze and Baha'i children share a classroom, with one teacher speaking only Arabic and one speaking only Hebrew. They learn each other's religions and celebrate everyone's holidays, in shortened form.
    • It began with one school in the north. Demand grew, and there are now at least four, two of them in Jerusalem. The kids grow up together "like normal societies should be."

The Kidron Valley and the north

  • The bus drove down the Kidron Valley, the same valley we crossed near its end two days earlier on the way to Masada. It runs toward the Dead Sea.
  • For thousands of years it was the city's geographic and psychological border. Nobody dared live on the far side.
    • The valley was once much deeper, with the slope and a wall above it.
  • The Hinnom Valley on the other side bounds the city to the west and south. The Kidron and Hinnom protected the city on the east, west and south.
  • There's no deep valley on the north. The Bible calls the north Jerusalem's weak point, and in 3,000 years of conquest up to the 1960s almost every invader came from the north.

The Temple Mount Sifting Project

  • Construction of the underground Al-Marwani Mosque on the Temple Mount (below the Al-Aqsa Mosque) brought bulldozers in, and the excavated earth was dumped into the Kidron Valley.

The oldest part of Jerusalem

Finds on display

  • The Priestly Blessing, found across the valley by Gabi Barkai.
    • "This is the oldest written form of the Bible that we have." It dates to about the 7th century BC and quotes the priestly blessing of Numbers 6 (Numbers 6:24–26), so copies of Numbers already existed before the Babylonian exile.
    • For about 150 years many liberal scholars have taught that most of the Old Testament was written during the exile to create a national identity, and that Moses and David never existed.
  • The Tel Dan Inscription (we had seen its find spot at Dan's gate, near Jeroboam's seat of judgment)
    • It was found in the early 1990s. An enemy king boasts of victory over Israel and calls the southern kingdom the "House of David," which is where the TV show gets its name.
    • Until then, David was treated as a legendary figure. Jeremy said the inscription dates to about 300 years after his reign, which shows his lasting influence. Finds like this are "the arguments that you use against modern scholarship" in apologetics for the Bible's reliability.
  • A menorah medallion (later period) and the three symbols of Judaism
    • Wherever Jews lived, in the land or in the diaspora (Priene in Turkey, Corinth in Greece, Thessalonica in Macedonia), they left the same three marks: the menorah, the shofar, and the etrog (sometimes shown with the lulav).
    • All three point to the temple. The menorah was its central focal point, the shofar was blown there daily, and the etrog and lulav belong to its festivals. "There is always a drawing of the Jewish people to the temple throughout their identity and their history."

    • The growing claim that the Jewish people have no claim here because the temple wasn't there is "a defiling of the image of Judaism," and it's a new argument.

  • An earring from the tomb of the Queen of Adiabene
    • A friend of Jeremy's found it while excavating a nearby parking lot. She was a queen who came to the land and converted to Judaism in the first century.

  • Purity tokens (Jeremy bought a replica)
    • Purity requirements rose sharply in Jerusalem, because you were preparing to enter the temple.
    • After ritually immersing, a pilgrim received a lump of clay stamped with a word meaning "pure unto God," an "entrance ticket" into the temple. They've been found here both as flat disks and, more often, as quickly stamped lumps handed out in bulk.
    • Coming down from the Galilee, Jesus' first act in Jerusalem would likely have been to immerse and purify.
    • There was a whole economy around the temple, and these were charged for.
    • On the high holy days, 1–1.5 million pilgrims swelled a city normally estimated at 150,000–250,000.

    • It was found by Jeremy's friend Eli Shukron. The high priest wore bells on his robe (Exodus 28:33–35).

Why the city was built here

  • The City of David isn't on the highest ground. The Mount of Olives, the temple hill and the western hill are all higher. The reason is water.
  • The Gihon Spring: Gihon means it gushes out in gulps, not a steady stream. It was the main water source for roughly 2,000–3,000 years.
  • A reconstruction shows the city from Solomon onward, since it includes a temple. In David's time there was only an altar and the palace.
    • The hilltop slopes diagonally, so from the palace roof at the top you could see every rooftop below. That fits David seeing Bathsheba.
  • The Kidron (east) and Hinnom (west) valleys meet in the south and run together toward the Dead Sea.
  • As the city grew it couldn't expand north (the temple), east or south (valleys), so it expanded to the western hill. The rich moved to this "upper west side," with the 6,000-square-foot mansions Jeremy had described.

Hezekiah's tunnel

  • After the kingdom split, a king of Judah prepared to rebel against Assyria. Assyria knew a king was rebelling when his taxes stopped arriving.
  • To prepare, the king (Hezekiah) walled in the newly settled area and moved the water source. A structure jutting out of the wall would tell an enemy, "Odds are you'll guess that it's the water."
  • Two teams of diggers started from opposite ends, about 2,800 years ago, without GPS, and met in the middle.
    • An inscription marks where they first heard each other and met. The original is in the Israel Museum, and there may be a copy near the tunnel's exit.
    • The tunnel zigzags so the two teams couldn't pass each other in parallel. They probably followed and widened a natural crack. Look for chisel marks.
  • The old outlet was destroyed so the enemy wouldn't know it existed. The water now flowed to a pool the city could reach.
  • Seven or eight hundred years later, King Herod built a huge pool there for ritual immersion before going up to the temple.
    • The group would sit on the steps down to the 2,000-year-old pool. Its location and size are known, and excavators are still trying to uncover the rest of it.
  • Q: People have argued with me that the City of David wasn't here but somewhere else. Where does that theory come from?
    • They don't want this to be the actual place, because then the Jewish people have a credible claim to the land. "You lie enough times, people will start to believe you."
    • With the 2,000-year-old pool and the original steps leading from it to the Temple Mount, "why would the City of David be anywhere else?"

Going "up" to Jerusalem

  • The Hebrew for pilgrimage, aliyah la-regel, means going up by foot. From any direction, the valleys mean you walk up into Jerusalem.
  • From the City of David you keep climbing to the temple. At the temple, every holier area had more steps, and fewer people could enter, up to the Holy of Holies. There only the high priest went, once a year on the Day of Atonement.
    • "It's not just a spiritual ascension, it's a physical one as well." When the psalmists look up to Jerusalem, they're describing real geography.

Warren's Shaft

  • In the 19th century, when the British were bribing the Ottomans for permission to dig, Charles Warren excavated here, found this shaft and assumed water was drawn up through it by bucket.
    • "It made absolutely no sense back then, it makes absolutely no sense today." Opher called it "useless and meaningless."
  • About a hundred years later, in the 20th century, the blocked passage was opened. It led to a pool or reservoir that people could walk around to fill their containers.
  • Most homes collected rainwater from the roof into cellars or cisterns to get through the winter. The Gihon Spring supplied the rest of the year.

Idols in Israelite houses

  • Overhead, archaeologists have found (though it isn't open to the public) the original 3,000-year-old ground level of the city and its houses. Eli Shukron, lead archaeologist here for about three decades, found about 90% of what's here, including the bell.
  • Even with Solomon's temple just above, the Israelite houses contained idols, such as big-bellied fertility figures you rubbed for luck.
    • "The idea of one God is great. But we believe in a few others." Figures like these were common in Israelite homes throughout the land.
  • The first two commandments address exactly this. After 400 years steeped in Egyptian paganism, the people believed in other gods. The ten plagues were also meant to teach the Israelites that there is only one God.
  • Through the whole First Temple period (about 400 years), God battled the people's belief in "the one God, and his wife, and a few others."
  • The exile and the temple's destruction purified the religion. God became more of a spiritual being, and idolatry was gone by the Second Temple period. "There are other ways to sin, obviously," but after that, "it literally is one God and nothing else."

The Canaanite pool

  • At the bottom of the stairs is one of the oldest things on the whole tour: a pool and channel probably made by the Canaanites, about 3,800–4,000 years old.
    • David took the city from the Jebusites, who had probably taken it from the Canaanites.
    • It carried water from the Gihon Spring to a small pool.
  • By David's time it was completely covered and the Jebusites were using a different system. The Israelites didn't know it existed.
  • It was cleared out only in the last five to eight years. Even the archaeologists aren't sure of all the water systems here.

John 9 — the man born blind

  • The disciples ask, "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents?" Jesus says neither: "that the works of God might be displayed in him" (John 9:1–3).
  • Jesus spits, makes mud, anoints the man's eyes and sends him to wash in Siloam ("Sent"). He comes back seeing (John 9:6–7).
  • It was the Sabbath, and that's the key to the whole story.

Why the Pharisees were confused

  • The Sabbath rule: you cannot create and you cannot destroy. Jesus made mud, and John mentions it three times on purpose.
    • "In the minds of the people, Jesus just created. He just broke the Sabbath."
  • It echoes the two schools of Pharisees discussed up north. The looser school prioritized preserving the person. The stricter school prioritized preserving the Sabbath.
  • Only some Pharisees objected, and even they were split:
    • One group said, "This man is not from God." They weren't calling him a sinner. They meant he wasn't sent by God as "we" are, which is closer to calling him a God-fearer (someone who worships God but isn't part of Judaism).
    • The other group called him a sinner.
  • The parents deflect ("He is of age, ask him") because confessing Jesus meant being put out of the synagogue (John 9:22). "We don't want to be kicked out of the synagogue, but you can throw our son out."

The healed man's testimony

  • Called in a second time and told Jesus is a sinner, he answers, "Whether he is a sinner I do not know. One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see" (John 9:24–25).
    • "It doesn't matter. I see the reality of this." The point Jesus keeps making is that God isn't limited by the boxes we try to put him in.
  • They revile him: "You are his disciple, but we are disciples of Moses" (John 9:28–29). He answers that no one has ever opened the eyes of a man born blind, and if Jesus weren't from God he could do nothing. They cast him out (John 9:30–34).
  • Jesus finds him: "Do you believe in the Son of Man?" "Who is he, sir, that I may believe?" "You have seen him, and it is he who is speaking to you." "Lord, I believe," and he worshiped him (John 9:35–38).
    • That's Jesus' focus. He opened the man's eyes so that he could see him.

John's "Quentin Tarantino" technique

  • Tarantino's movies show a glimpse of the ending at the start, and then the whole film explains how things got there. John does the same.
  • Here, the disciples' opening question ("why was he born blind?") is answered at the end: so that the glory of God could be revealed "in him, to him, and through him."
  • John does the same with the cleansing of the temple. Matthew, Mark and Luke place it right after the triumphal entry in the final week. John places it at the very start of Jesus' ministry (John 2:13–17).
    • It isn't necessarily when it happened. He puts it up front because that's the event that made the Sadducees enact the plan that put Jesus on the cross. Many wanted to kill Jesus, but "this is the one that actually succeeds."

"Are we also blind?"

  • Jesus: "For judgment I came into this world, that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind." Some Pharisees ask, "Are we also blind?" (John 9:39–40)
    • They're curious, not rejecting him outright. They see their own deficiency and realize their theology might be wrong, because they can't explain what happened.
  • Jesus: "If you were blind, you would have no guilt; but now that you say, 'We see,' your guilt remains" (John 9:41).
    • The very fact that they're questioning it shows they know the answer.

Application

  • Sometimes God removes the things in our lives that make us blind "because he simply just wants us to see." Sometimes those things are placed in our path ahead of time so that we have a testimony at the end.
  • "Sometimes God has to deconstruct our theology to draw us close to him."
    • That's hard today, when "deconstruction" has become a hot topic and a community for people who have walked away from faith on Instagram and TikTok.
    • But we all have preconceived notions, and sometimes God reveals himself so powerfully that all we can say is, "Maybe I got it wrong from the get-go. Maybe God is bigger than I originally thought. Maybe that shoebox I put him in isn't exactly what holds the infinite creator of creation."

The temple through the centuries (viewed from the east, as from the Mount of Olives)

  • Solomon's Temple was destroyed by the Babylonians in 586 BC and rebuilt about 70 years later, in 516 BC.
    • The rebuilt temple was "kind of pathetic." Those old enough to remember Solomon's Temple wept bitterly, because it was nothing by comparison (Ezra 3:12).
  • The Hasmoneans were the first to really invest in it, enlarging the building and its courtyards.
  • In the first century BC, Herod the Great, Rome's client king, expanded it enormously.
    • He also built temples to the Caesars, three of them to Caesar Augustus. The temple project kept the Jewish populace happy.
    • It was adorned in gold. "If you have not looked upon Herod the Great's temple in your lifetime, you have not seen a truly beautiful thing."
  • The Antonia Fortress on the temple's corner began as a Greek fortress under the Seleucids, and the Romans greatly expanded it.
  • Solomon's Portico, the colonnade along the edge, is where Jesus walked and talked with people, especially at the festivals (John 10:23).

Seleucids, Ptolemies and Hasmoneans

  • After Alexander the Great, two dynasties fought over the land: the Seleucids, based in Syria, and the Ptolemies, based in Egypt. Things changed every time the land changed hands.
  • The Ptolemies were loose with the people. The Seleucids were much stricter.
  • That strictness led to the revolt that set up the Hasmonean dynasty against its Greek overlords. The Seleucids were being weakened by the relationship between the Ptolemies and Rome, so they tried to hold the city as tightly as they could, and built the Antonia.

The temple courts

  • The outer court was open to anyone. Josephus describes a Court of the Women and a Court of the Gentiles in it.
    • The Israel Museum holds one of the two known inscriptions marking how far Gentiles could go. The other is in the Istanbul museum.
  • The inner court was for Israel only. The Holy Place was for the Levites. The Holy of Holies was for the high priest alone.
  • The Pools of Bethesda lie to the north, where St. Anne's Church stands today. Jesus healed the man lying on his mat there, who was waiting for the waters to be stirred (John 5:1–9).
    • "An angel of the Lord disturbs it, which they assume to be some type of earthquake or something like that."

Walls

  • The Third Wall, which wraps around the north side to the three towers, did not exist in Jesus' day. It was built to prepare for Titus, who was bringing his forces down from the war in the Galilee to destroy the temple.
    • To picture Jesus' Jerusalem, mentally remove this wall. There were still parts of the city outside the walls.
  • The older-looking houses stand on Mount Zion, a separate hill within Jerusalem.
    • When the northern kingdom fell to Assyria in 722 BC, many refugees fled south. Hezekiah settled them on this hill, and with Sennacherib coming, he built a wall cutting through the city: the Broad Wall.
    • The Broad Wall isn't on the model, because it lies below the first-century level.
  • In today's Jerusalem, lines of paving mark the ancient walls: black brick for the first-century Hasmonean walls, and red brick for Hezekiah's walls. "As you're walking, you'll sometimes see pavement of red brick and black brick that goes in a line."
  • The group would see the same area from the Mount of Olives the next day, across the Kidron.

Three valleys, two hills (viewed from the south)

  • The Kidron Valley runs on the east. The Hinnom Valley (Gehinnom) runs on the south.
    • The Hinnom became equated with hell. Tradition holds it served as the garbage dump, so people avoided it, and it became a place of pagan ritual and burning.
    • Some sources place King Manasseh's child sacrifices to Molech here. That sin angered the Lord so much that even Josiah's righteousness couldn't stay his hand (2 Kings 23:26).
    • When Jesus speaks of hell in a Hebraic mindset, this is the term he uses. That ties back to the earlier teaching up north on Hades and Tartarus in the Greek world.
  • The third, smaller valley is the Tyropoeon, which divides the two hills. Modern development makes the two hills hard to see today.
    • The first hill is the City of David. The second hill is Hezekiah's city. The city later expanded beyond them.

The Pool of Siloam and the City of David on the model

  • The big open square at the bottom is the Pool of Siloam, with the pilgrims' path running up from it, which Opher pointed out to us.
    • The group came out of Hezekiah's Tunnel in the drainage channel area under the bridges, near the second-to-last bridge.
    • Archaeology puts the real pool a few meters to the left, but Josephus got the general location right.
  • It was the largest man-made mikveh in the Jewish world, built or dedicated for that purpose around the first centuries BC and AD.
    • The Pool of Bethesda was bigger and deeper, but it was not a mikveh.
  • The City of David was a very small part of first-century Jerusalem, maybe 10,000 of the 150,000–250,000 people.
    • Archaeologists can't determine much, because so much was destroyed or slid into the Kidron Valley.
  • "Archaeology is the science of destruction." You have to destroy later layers to get to earlier ones. Here, erosion and earthquakes stripped away the later layers, which preserved the earlier ones.
    • That's why we saw few Roman remains that morning. They were washed away or removed to expose the Iron Age and earlier.

The tomb of David

  • A nefesh (tomb monument) sits by the southern steps.
  • There are two proposals for David's tomb: just outside Hezekiah's city, or right here south of the Temple Mount. Most archaeologists favor here.
    • David established himself in this area, and being buried far outside the city would have been a big deal. David bought the temple hill, but Solomon developed it, so this spot would have been just outside the city.
    • Peter's Pentecost sermon in Acts 2 alludes to it (Acts 2:29). Jeremy would explain the language at the Southern Steps. It may show where the sermon took place.

Gates and Herod's palace (viewed from the west)

  • Jerusalem can't be attacked from the east, south or west because of the valleys, so invasions always came from the north.
    • Herod built strongholds at two choke points to push enemies where he wanted them: the Gennath Gate and the Jaffa Gate.
    • Three towers guarded the north side of his palace, and the palace lay just south of them.

Where Jesus was tried

  • Pilate was "in the palace." Tradition puts him at the Antonia Fortress, but that doesn't fit how Roman prefects and procurators operated.
  • Pilate came to Jerusalem at Passover for crowd control.
    • Passover celebrated the firstborn of Israel being passed over and Israel's release from foreign oppression. In the first century, the foreign oppressor was Rome. Can you see why there'd be tension in the Roman ranks?
    • He stayed in Herod's palace, where the praetorium was and from which he could run things, not in the Antonia with the soldiers.
  • Why the Antonia tradition is so strong:
    • It arose in the medieval period, largely through the Catholic Church, because the Antonia was the quickest route from the Christian Quarter. Herod's palace sits in today's Armenian Quarter, which in the Crusader era was the Jewish Quarter, and communities didn't mix.
    • Traditions were set up throughout the land to control where pilgrims went, to keep them safe and to keep the money within a community. That's why several sites claim to be Bethany beyond the Jordan. "It's like what Opher said, why we have multiple different traditions."
  • The route on the model:
    • Jesus was arrested in Gethsemane on the Mount of Olives. Gat Shemanim means the place of the olive press, where oil was made from the mount's olives.
    • He was brought to Annas, Caiaphas and the Sadducees, with some Pharisees. Their excavated houses in today's Jewish Quarter, near the theater, were on the next day's itinerary.
    • Then he went to Pilate at Herod's palace. Pilate learned Jesus was a Galilean and sent him to Herod Antipas, who was in town.
    • Josephus says the Herods stayed in the houses of their forefathers, the Hasmoneans, so Antipas was in the Hasmonean palace with the two towers.
    • Antipas did nothing and sent him back to Pilate, who sent him to Golgotha (Luke 23:6–12).

Where was Golgotha?

  • The Church of the Holy Sepulchre has the stronger case, both archaeologically and by tradition.
    • In the 1800s Protestants were uncomfortable with it. It's shared by several churches, mainly the Catholic, Greek Orthodox, Syriac and Ethiopian churches, which don't get along, and fistfights have broken out.
    • A Muslim family holds the keys so the church can be locked when things get bad.
    • The "immovable ladder" has sat in the same place for centuries (dated sketches show it). It can't be moved unless every group agrees.
  • So 19th-century explorers proposed another site: "Ah, this rock looks like a skull. This must be the place."
  • Golgotha was a place of common execution, and crucifixions happened right outside the city gates, where the most people would pass.
  • Jesus was buried in a garden, so much so that Mary mistook him for the gardener (John 20:15). The Gennath Gate stands right by the Church of the Holy Sepulchre.
    • The rocky area there was a quarry. Quarries were later used for tombs, and since this was outside the wall at the time, burial there didn't affect the city's purity.

Passover, calendars and the crowds

  • "There's no such thing as nominal Judaism today. There's no such thing as nominal Judaism inside the first century." Groups celebrated differently and followed different calendars.
  • In Luke, Jesus eats the Passover lamb the night he's betrayed (Luke 22:15). In John, the lambs are being sacrificed while he's dying.
    • This isn't a contradiction. Depending on whether you followed a lunar or solar calendar, you sacrificed on a different day.
    • Western Christians and the Greek Orthodox celebrate Easter on different days for the same kind of reason.
  • The normal population was at most 150,000–250,000. Josephus says it swelled to 1–1.5 million at Passover.
    • Pilgrims had to stay outside the city. Jesus and his disciples camped on the Mount of Olives, like everyone else.

Crucifixion

  • Crucifixions were placed right outside the gate so the arriving crowds would see them. "Crucifixion is crowd control."
  • The men crucified with Jesus weren't thieves. The Greek word is lēstēs, revolutionaries.
    • Josephus uses the word again and again for his countrymen in the Great Revolt.
    • Barabbas, whose place Jesus took, is described with the same word, and there it's translated correctly (murderer, revolutionary).
    • The King James Version was the first mass-printed Bible, and James's reign faced civil strife between Catholics and Protestants, including the Gunpowder Plot and Guy Fawkes.
    • One of the men on the cross is forgiven: "Today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43). "If you're King James... do you want a revolutionary, somebody who rises up against the authority of the land, to be able to find salvation? No." So, Jeremy said, the translators used a far less common meaning, "thieves."
  • Other messianic hopefuls
    • Acts 5 mentions others who rose up.
    • Paul was arrested at the temple after a Nazirite vow and mistaken for "the Egyptian" (Acts 21:38). Josephus says the Egyptian raised a force (Jeremy said 5,000), fought the Romans, lost and retreated into the desert, with people following him in the hope he'd overthrow Rome.
    • Rome moved against anyone who might attract a following, and crucifixions along the road made the point to everyone entering the city.
    • After Spartacus' slave revolt, Rome crucified huge numbers of slaves along the roads as a warning.
  • Crucifixion was done at eye level, not hoisted high, so onlookers would witness the suffering up close.
    • The Romans were proud of it and left behind many works about it.
    • It could last a long time. Josephus tells of finding three childhood friends who had been on crosses for days; he asked the emperor to take them down, and only one survived even with care.
  • Before the Sabbath of Passover, one of the holiest Sabbaths of the year, the leaders asked Pilate to have the men taken down. Their legs were broken to hasten death (John 19:31–33).
    • Jesus was already dead because of the brutality he'd suffered at Pilate's hands before the cross.
    • The men beside him died "relatively mercifully" compared to most of those Rome crucified.
  • "One of the biggest identifiers of Jesus as a Jewish man in the first century is the fact that he hung on a Roman cross."

Opher explained Israel's parliamentary system.

How Israelis vote

  • Every citizen 18 and up votes on the same day. Residents of Area A aren't Israeli citizens and don't vote.
  • The Knesset (parliament) has 120 seats. The total of valid votes sets how many votes one seat takes, and each party gets seats by its share.
  • You vote for a party, not a person. There are dozens of parties to choose from, around 70 this year.
  • Each party submits a numbered list of up to 120 candidates. If a party wins 5% (6 seats), its top six names get in. One party never wins everything, because "we got two Jews, we got three opinions."

Building a coalition (Opher's mock election on the bus)

  • Opher cast group members as party leaders ("King Opher" with 25% on a "benevolent dictator" platform, plus a women's party, a young people's party, a sunscreen party and others) to show how it works.
  • A government needs a majority of at least 61 seats. Usually the largest party negotiates with the others to form a coalition. Winning the most seats alone "doesn't mean anything."
  • The small party that gets you from 60 to 61 holds all the power. "The fact that you're a small party does not mean that you have less power. Sometimes it means you have all the power."
  • A big coalition (for example, 83 seats) is stable, because a party can leave and you still have 61. The last few governments had only 61–64, which gave five- or six-seat parties enormous leverage.
  • He said the ultra-religious parties have played the system best in Israel's almost 80 years.
    • They focus on funding for their Torah students and exemptions from military service, and will support either side on other issues in exchange.
    • With around 12 seats at most, they've been in government for all but about eight years.
  • The leader has 30 days to form a coalition, plus 30 more if talks are hard. If that fails, the country goes to new elections.
  • The Knesset is elected for four years. If a coalition partner walks out and the government can't "reshuffle the deck" to reach 61, it's new elections again.
    • Opher said nearly every government has fallen before its full four years, usually when a small party votes with the opposition.
  • The opposition can still pass laws. For example, an opposition member passed a law setting a minimum age and an awareness course for electric bikes after seeing children injured. Opposition laws pass on non-ideological issues, not the big ones that divide Israel.

The president

  • The president's role is representative, "kinda like the Queen of England."
  • The president is elected by the Knesset, not the public, and is usually a senior, well-liked longtime member.
  • A candidate needs the backing of 10% of the Knesset (12 members) to be nominated.

Questions

  • Q: How soon can a coalition fall apart?
    • It could be as little as two weeks.
  • Q: Isn't that manipulation?
    • Opher said the ultra-Orthodox are about 10% of the population but can secure an outsized share of the budget. It isn't bribery; it's how the coalition system works.
  • Q: Are there term limits for prime minister?
    • No. Each party chooses its own list. If a party leader heads the list, wins the most seats and forms a coalition, he's prime minister again, however many times that happens.

Standing on the first-century street

  • The group stood at first-century level, 30–40 feet below today's streets by the Western Wall.

Why the stones came down

  • Herod covered the temple in gold. Gold melts at a relatively low temperature, so when the temple burned, the gold melted down into it.
  • You can spot Herod's masonry by its "little square inside a square" (a flat, framed face). There's no mortar between the blocks. They're held together, even 2,000 years later, by precise engineering.
  • The melted gold ran into the joints. After the fire, the Romans pried the stones apart to get the gold and threw them over the side.
    • "Guys, this is the remains of the temple up above." One stone crashed straight through the street pavement.
  • The temple itself stood about as tall as three Domes of the Rock stacked on top of each other.
  • An arch once shaded this busy spot and served as a way into and out of the temple. It's called Robinson's Arch, after the man who identified it.

Jesus and the money changers

  • The scene is usually placed on top of the Temple Mount, but the Mishnah says money couldn't change hands up there. This street was still part of the temple complex, so it's the natural spot.
  • The rooms along the street were exchange shops. They connect directly back to the houses of the high priests, the 6,000-square-foot mansions we would visit the next day.
  • The half-shekel tax
    • Every man paid a half shekel a year for the temple's upkeep, and it had to be paid in Tyrian shekels.
    • The Tyrian shekel had Hercules on one side and an eagle on the other. It was a pagan coin with graven images.
    • Jewish-minted coins avoided images that could be worshiped. They used an anchor, a geometric pattern, a flower or an amphora.
    • The Tyrian shekel was required because it had the highest silver purity in the ancient world. Elsewhere, coins were gradually mixed with cheaper metals, like U.S. quarters that used to be silver and pennies that used to be copper.
    • Nobody carried these coins day to day. They were imported. "Imagine if, in order to get into Freedom Church on a Sunday, Pastor Benito said, I want you to get me X amount of Guatemalan pesos." And he's the only one in town who has them, at his own exchange rate.
  • Sacrificial animals
    • Passover required an unblemished lamb. A lamb wasn't going to survive a seven- or eight-day walk from the Galilee unblemished. "Sheep are stupid. There's a reason why we are likened to sheep inside the Bible again and again."

What the Western Wall is

  • The southern wall of the Temple Mount runs along the Davidson Center, and the corner there is where the Western Wall begins.
  • The Western Wall is Judaism's holiest site, "at least nowadays."
  • For a long time only a narrow strip of the wall was accessible to Jews, so traditions formed around praying there.
    • Jews are supposed to pray toward the Holy of Holies, which was up where the golden Dome of the Rock stands. At the Western Wall that means praying up and a little to the left.
    • Many people just stare at the wall, thinking it's the temple's wall. "It's not. Let's get that clear once and for all." It's a retaining wall that held up the platform the temple stood on.
    • In the first century the street along it was basically a shopping center. Nobody prayed at the wall, because they had the temple.
  • Today it's considered the largest open-air synagogue in the world, so men (the northern section) and women (the southern section) are separated.
    • Shoulders and knees must be covered, and men need a head covering. Paper hats and kippot are handed out, and you return them when you leave.

"Judaism for Dummies": the denominations

  • Nobody used to dress the way the ultra-Orthodox dress today. "This is just a tradition."
  • Modernization in 18th- and 19th-century Europe (the Industrial Revolution, and later radio, TV and the internet) drew three main reactions:
    • Ultra-Orthodox: modernity is the worst thing that could happen, so enclose the community, keep modern things away from the children and hold to the forefathers' way of life.
    • Secular: modernity is the best thing. "People like myself." No need for a kippah to show you're Jewish. You pick and choose traditions, like a Passover meal with family, maybe fasting on Yom Kippur, "but the following day I might eat a bacon cheeseburger."
    • Orthodox: traditional life and modern life can go together. They shut off the TV and all electrical appliances for the roughly 25 hours of Shabbat, pray three times a day and wear a kippah, but have cell phones and the rest.
  • Later, especially in the U.S., Jews in big cities often didn't have a synagogue within walking distance. To stay connected and keep Judaism alive for their children, they drove to synagogue on Shabbat. That became roughly Conservative Judaism, between Orthodox and secular.
  • Every denomination today falls somewhere between the very strict ultra-Orthodox and the not-strict secular.

Religious dress

  • Religious Jews wear an undergarment with fringes (tzitzit), sometimes tucked in. That's the "hem" the woman with the bleeding touched on Jesus' garment (Matthew 9:20).
  • The black suits

    • Some rabbis permit electric razors and some don't. The strictest only trim with scissors.
    • Remember Samson, who lost his hair and his connection to God? "We want to show that we never lose our connection," so a strand is left above the cheekbone, visible even under a hat. It's left from the first haircut, around age five, and a lifetime of twirling it makes it curl.
  • Women's hair
    • A woman's hair is considered one of her most attractive features.
    • Once married, a religious woman shows her hair only to her husband and immediate family. She covers it or wears a wig.
  • Hats
    • Each ultra-Orthodox community and rabbi has its own style. There's a whole book on ultra-Orthodox dress, with an entire chapter on hats.
    • Hats are among their most expensive clothing, and some cost $3,000–$5,000. On flights, they wear a small kippah and keep the hat in a special box.

Herod's platform

  • Herod came from a family that converted to Judaism, and Jews always looked down on him. He wanted to be the greatest Jewish builder in history, "even bigger than King Solomon himself."
  • His design was too big for the mountain, so he undertook one of the biggest building projects in the history of the land and of the Roman Empire.

    • In the south the slope dropped away. He used the Roman solution of a series of arches, like the one over our heads, to hold up an artificial plaza.
    • It's like Caesarea, where Herod built a port with no natural harbor.
  • Massive retaining walls held the platform, and on it stood probably the biggest temple in the Roman world, one of the great achievements of the empire.
  • Building began around 20 BC ("depends where you ask"), and it was destroyed in AD 70. Jesus predicted its destruction just before his execution under Pontius Pilate.

First-century burial

  • In the Old Testament a person was "gathered to his fathers." The body was laid on a bench in the family tomb, and a year later the bones were swept into a central pit with generations of relatives.
  • In the New Testament period the bones instead went into an ossuary, a stone box (stone keeps purity), and the ossuaries were stored in a room of the tomb for generations.
  • This produced the kokhim-style tomb, shaped like a hand: a preparation chamber in the palm, the entrance at the wrist, and burial slots like fingers.
  • Ossuaries only need to be as long as the femur (the longest bone) and as wide as the hip bone (the widest).
  • Many are inscribed with names, including biblical ones. The ossuary of "Miriam, daughter of Caiaphas" is in the Israel Museum.
    • First-century names came from a small pool (Miriam is Mary), so identifiers were added. Jesus is Yeshua (Joshua), a common name, hence "Jesus, son of Joseph, of Nazareth."

Jesus' burial

  • The legs of the men crucified next to Jesus were broken to hurry their deaths before sundown, because it was a Passover Sabbath, one of the holiest of the year.
  • The body was prepared but they ran out of time. The women planned to return at first light Sunday, since Saturday night was too dark.
  • Joseph of Arimathea was very wealthy and gave Jesus his own newly cut tomb.
    • The first person buried in a tomb is the one it is always associated with.
    • Executed criminals were usually dumped in a field. Jewish law barred burying them in the family tomb, and a new tomb was hugely expensive. That is probably what happened to the two men beside Jesus.
    • Joseph could petition Pilate for the body, which shows his influence. Joseph and Nicodemus were leading, wealthy Pharisees and disciples of Jesus.
    • He says Josephus names two families spared in the Great Revolt's destruction of the city: the sons of Joseph and of Nicodemus, "the hand of the providence of God on them."

The stone: square, not round

  • Only three tombs in Jerusalem have a circular rolling stone, and all belonged to royalty: cousins of Herod the Great (we would see it later that day), the queen of Adiabene, and a prince.
  • Joseph was rich but not royal, so the stone was most likely square, the more common kind. It was easier and cheaper to cut, since a round stone must be sanded to roll. The first-century tomb in the Holy Sepulchre has an entrance cut for a square stone.
  • "Rolled" still fits: ropes were tied on each side, and three or four people pulled it end over end.
  • A round stone gave anyone easy access. That's why the women ask, "Who will roll away the stone?" (Mark 16:3).
  • The synoptics say it was rolled away. John says it was taken away, as if tossed aside, which shows the power of what happened to a stone that was sealed in like a cork (John 20:1).
  • In the tombs you still have to bend down to look in, just as the disciples bent and saw where he had lain (John 20:5, 11–12).

Why they didn't recognize him

  • Mary asks the "gardener" what he has done with the body (John 20:15).
  • His own story: he grew up believing in healing as a pastor's son, but the first time he actually saw a leg miraculously healed, "it rocked me to my core."
  • The disciples had seen a mangled body on the cross. Three days later they weren't expecting to see him, like meeting someone you know in a place you don't expect.
  • Jesus looked like any other first-century Jew. That's why Judas had to identify him with a kiss, even though he'd been teaching in the temple every day that week.
  • Recognition came when he showed what they expected, his wounds, to Thomas (John 20:27). On the road to Emmaus they said, "Did not our hearts burn within us?" (Luke 24:32).
    • "Their spirits knew the calling of their master... even if the senses denied it." Our souls will know the power of the Spirit even when our senses deny it.

Jesus wept twice on this mountain

  • Dominus Flevit means "the Lord wept."
  • The better-known time is in Gethsemane. He tells the disciples to stay awake, and three times they fall asleep.
    • They'd just had the Passover meal with two to four cups of wine. He agrees with the modern four, though some scholars say two. "Some holidays... you are commanded to enjoy the fruit of the vine."
  • The first time is here, around the Olivet Discourse, weeping over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41–44). He weeps not for the buildings but for the hearts of people, especially those claiming loyalty to him, who could have changed course and chose not to.
  • He believes Theophilus, the recipient of Luke's gospel, was the second-to-last high priest before the Great Revolt. Luke was showing him that he had the answer to the coming catastrophe.
  • War was inevitable from the time Judas of Gamla (Judas the Galilean) rose up after Herod the Great's death. It took about 70 years to come.
    • He compares it to the American Civil War, foreseen from the 1830s, and to World War II after Versailles: "You don't have the instability, the chaos, the abuses... and not have civil war."

"His only begotten Son" and the binding of Isaac

  • "Begotten" in John 3:16 is deliberate Old Testament language. Isaac was the begotten son, the one the promise rested on, though Abraham also had Ishmael.
  • Isaac's life centers on one story: Abraham offering him on Mount Moriah (Genesis 22). Mount Moriah, the older name before Zion, is the Temple Mount across the valley.
  • Judaism calls it the Akedah, "the binding of Isaac," and the binding is the key to the story.
  • Media portrays a little boy confused about the sacrifice, but:
    • The young men left with the animals are called by the same word used for Isaac, which suggests a young man of about 17 or older.
    • Life expectancy then ran into the hundreds, so he was still young. Early Jewish tradition puts Isaac at about 33.
    • Abraham was advanced in years, and Isaac carried the wood because he was the stronger.
  • Abraham binds him so he won't flinch and ruin the sacrifice. If Isaac hadn't been willing, he could have overpowered his father and run.
    • "This is not just a test of Abraham. It's a test of both of them." Isaac submits because the Lord commanded it.
  • Isaac is said to be 33, the same age as Jesus, and both are called the begotten son to draw a direct parallel.

Jesus chose to stay

  • The arresting party came with torches, so Jesus would have seen them coming and had a head start.
  • Behind the hotel at the top of the Mount of Olives, about 15 minutes' walk puts you in the Judean desert. "You walk an hour, nobody will find you." Jesus chose to confront them instead.
  • Judas' kiss was needed because Jesus looked like everyone else, and the hillside was full of pilgrims camping for Passover.
    • Line Jesus up with 99 Gentiles and you could pick him out. Line him up with 99 Jews and "we would not be able to distinguish who Jesus was."

Peter's sword and Malchus' ear

  • Peter is not a wild man flailing. He cuts off the ear on purpose, to show he knows how to use a sword: "come at me."
  • These were temple guards, not Roman soldiers, armed with daggers rather than swords. Rome, fearing revolt, kept good weapons away from any organized force.
    • He compares it to the US giving allies older equipment, such as Vietnam-era M16A2s, dated police handguns, and not its most advanced F-35 even for Israel.
  • Malchus was the high priest's servant (John 18:10).
    • Under Herod the high priesthood no longer passed father to son from Aaron; it went to the highest bidder, "a mafia" (the wealth shows in the priestly houses of the Wohl Museum).
    • To learn the business you sold yourself into service to the chief priest, "an unpaid intern, basically."
    • A priest had to be physically whole: all ten fingers and toes, both nostrils, both eyes, both ears. Cutting off the ear ended Malchus' investment and his career.
    • A deliberate strike at the ear, rather than the head, takes skill: "Peter knows what he's doing."
  • Jesus intervenes: he could call down legions of angels, yet he submits to the Father's will (Matthew 26:53). By sunrise he is on a cross, dead within hours.

Why the tradition is early

  • The church dates to the same time as the Church of the Nativity, about 1,700 years old. It was built after Christianity was legalized, when Helena came and asked local Christians where these events happened.
  • It is a pre-Byzantine tradition, kept while being Christian was still illegal and before pilgrimage existed, so it carries weight.
    • Once Byzantine pilgrimage began there was money in it, and locals said "It happened here. Now give me $20," like paying $20 to be baptized today.

The archaeology

  • Early church writings say that after Rome took the city in AD 136, at the end of the Bar Kokhba revolt, Hadrian utterly destroyed the tomb and built a pagan temple over it.
  • In the basement, closed to the public (he has been down many times), the church rests on pillars from a pagan temple.
    • Holy places stay holy: the Temple Mount became a temple to Zeus when the city was renamed Aelia Capitolina.
  • The Edicule, the "little house" in the Greek Orthodox area under the dome, is venerated as Jesus' tomb. Archaeologically it can't be, because the tomb was destroyed. "Somewhere within eyeshot is where it happened."
  • It checks every box:
    • First-century tombs here mean it was outside the city, since you can't have tombs inside.
    • It was a place of burial, right along the main road into the city from the north, where crucifixions would be seen.
    • Early sources say crucifixion and burial were feet apart, and the Gennath (garden) Gate points to a garden nearby.

The Garden Tomb comparison

  • The Garden Tomb, outside today's Old City, is where Protestants felt more comfortable. It has no historical reliability, and even its guides say it is a later tradition.
  • Its venerated tomb is a seventh-century BC tomb. They claim Joseph of Arimathea reworked it, but the gospels say it was a newly cut tomb.

Six churches in one

  • Six denominations share the church, and each uses its territory to connect itself to the story:
    • The chapel just inside to the right is claimed as Golgotha, the rock of the crucifixion. That is later speculation.
    • A Russian Orthodox pillar is claimed as the scourging pillar. Pilgrims put their ears to it to hear Jesus' cries, though the scourging happened at Herod's palace. He calls it "pseudo-faith."
    • The stone of anointing, where the body was supposedly prepared, is only about 300 years old.
  • The immovable ladder has been in the same spot at least since 1536, as early explorers' sketches show. It proves the denominations don't get along.
  • Needed restoration was under way; parts of the church had been falling apart. Each church may work in its own area.
  • Crusader-era graffiti is carved near the entrance.

The first-century tomb

  • At the back is a true first-century kokhim tomb, "a perfect example" of what Jesus' tomb looked like.
    • The entrance edge is cut for a square stone.
    • You step down into the central preparation area. Because they ran out of time, Jesus' body was left there, not yet placed in a niche. It would have gone into the niche on Sunday, "however, his body is not there."
    • Today it's lit by candles; then it would have been an oil lamp.
  • Q: The niches in the wall?
    • That's where the body was placed to decompose for a year; then the bones went into ossuaries like those seen that morning.
  • Q: So the stone was rolled?
    • Same verb: a rope tied on each side, pulled so it rolled.
  • Q: Why were Roman soldiers guarding the tomb?
    • They feared someone would steal the body.

Armenian chapel and the Holy Fire

  • An Armenian Orthodox chapel caught fire but its altar survived. It is considered miraculous and has stood there for a few hundred years. The chapel is still used on holidays such as Easter.
  • The Holy Fire ceremony before Easter happens in the central area. Orthodox and Catholic leaders light it and pass the flame through the building.
    • Rivalry over who sits in front and who lights it sometimes ends in fistfights.
    • "I have seen more fights, more contention inside this building than anywhere else... I see Christians fight Christians more than anybody else."
  • The line for the Edicule is normally hours long, like the Church of the Nativity. With no line today, he let us go in.
